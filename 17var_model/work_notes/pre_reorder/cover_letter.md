# Cover Letter: Submission to the Journal of Medical Internet Research (JMIR)

*[Date] · To the Editor-in-Chief, Journal of Medical Internet Research*

Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed Original Paper, "A Survey-Based Machine Learning Depression Screener Developed in Korea and Externally Validated in the United States Across the COVID-19 Period: Algorithm Development and Validation Study," for publication in JMIR.

**What the study does.** The study develops a machine-learning depression screener (PHQ-9 ≥10) in the Korea Community Health Survey (n≈2.05 million) and externally validates it, without refitting, in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) across National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS)-defined pre- and post-COVID-19 cycles. To the authors' knowledge this is among the first depression-screening models validated across country, health system, and the pandemic distribution shift using an identically defined outcome in both surveys.

**Why it matters for JMIR's readership.** Most published survey-based depression models report internal validation only and, in Korean data, achieve internal AUROCs up to ~0.90 while relying on predictors (eg, perceived stress, depressed-mood items) that are near-tautological with the outcome. The present study instead asks the question that matters for any deployable digital screener: does the model transport? Discrimination remains clinically useful but lower on external data (AUROC ~0.77); calibration drifts yet is recoverable, on point estimates, by lightweight intercept/slope recalibration; and, critically, this ~0.77 external ceiling withstood every leakage-controlled attempt made to raise it, including matching sample size and correcting covariate shift. The ceiling is therefore best read as a limit of the transportable predictor set rather than as a modeling failure. That reading rests on an in-domain oracle (fitting NHANES on its own labels reaches only ~0.77 across the learners tested, whereas the same predictors reach 0.81-0.82 within Korea at matched sample size), which is an empirical benchmark rather than a proof of a maximum, and a registry of 2,199 catalogued optimization runs; predictor-importance rank order is also highly concordant across surveys (Spearman ρ≈0.97-0.99). A parsimonious six-item version retains most of the external discrimination, supporting a low-burden web/mobile triage tool, squarely within JMIR's scope of internet-based health services.

**Methodological rigor and transparency.** The work follows TRIPOD+AI (2024; an item-by-item mapping is enclosed as Multimedia Appendix 2, and the official checklist form will be uploaded with the submission) with PROBAST risk-of-bias self-assessment. The harmonized predictor set is restricted to items both surveys measure in a form reducible to a common definition, and the instrument differences that remain are reported item by item. Three of the seventeen entered through a dated protocol amendment, written while the study was under way and locked before any model using the amended set was fitted; the amendment is summarized in English in Multimedia Appendix 1, with the unmodified Korean original released with the code, the added items were chosen on survey availability and non-circularity rather than on any outcome association, and the contribution of those items is reported as a robustness analysis rather than a primary result. Fitting, tuning and algorithm selection for the primary model used development-data cross-validation only, the in-domain oracle being a deliberate diagnostic exception, and the deployment item count follows the analysis plan's margin applied inside the development data; a smaller-margin seven-item variant is reported and labeled exploratory, the 0.01 margin being post hoc while the plan fixed 0.02, and the inferential 14-versus-17-predictor comparison gave each model its own hyperparameter search, so that it is not confounded by tuning effort, while the predictor-set variants tabulated in the appendix share one common configuration. An adversarial data-integrity audit and subsequent peer review identified seven data-processing and harmonization defects, all corrected in the primary analysis, each described in the data-integrity audit paragraph of External Validation and Sensitivity Analyses, and six harmonization corrections are quantified one by one in Multimedia Appendix 1 Table S7, the three audit corrections not being separable in that way. The analysis code, variable-harmonization dictionary, model card, and protocol/pre-specification documents are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request, subject to survey data-use restrictions; generative AI tools assisted with code and drafting under full author verification.

**Declarations.** This manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and all authors approve submission. The authors declare no competing interests. The study uses de-identified secondary survey data; NHANES is openly available, while raw KCHS microdata are access-controlled and not redistributable.

This study should interest JMIR readers working on scalable digital mental-health screening and on the real-world transportability of clinical AI. Reviewers with expertise in clinical prediction modeling, survey epidemiology, and digital mental health would be well suited; no reviewers are excluded.
