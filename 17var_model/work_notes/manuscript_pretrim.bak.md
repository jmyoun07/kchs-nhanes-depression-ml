## Abstract

**Background:** Depression is a leading cause of disability, and scalable, low-cost screening is needed. Most machine learning (ML) depression models from national surveys report internal validation only; whether such models transport to another country and remain stable across the COVID-19 shift is rarely tested.

**Objective:** To develop an ML model for current depression (Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9] ≥10) in the Korea Community Health Survey (KCHS), externally validate it in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) across the pre- and post-COVID-19 periods as the engine for a digital triage tool, and determine whether any external shortfall is closable by modeling.

**Methods:** Elastic net, random forest and gradient boosting were developed in KCHS (2017-2025; pooled n=2,048,510; cluster-level split into a 500,000-record training set and a 100,001-record hold-out sharing no cluster) using a harmonized 17-predictor set shared with NHANES, one item of which is unavailable in the pre-pandemic NHANES file. Primary-model fitting, tuning and selection used KCHS-internal cross-validation only; the trained model was applied to NHANES without refitting. Evaluation was complex-survey-weighted with rescaled cluster-bootstrap CIs, plus Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) and subgroup fairness. Performance was contrasted between the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS)-defined pre-pandemic (n=7,888) and post-pandemic (n=5,210) cycles. A leakage-controlled run registry and an in-domain oracle fitted on NHANES labels characterized attainable external discrimination.

**Results:** Gradient boosting (LightGBM) was primary. Internal area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in clusters never used for training was 0.836 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.827-0.846), meeting the ≥0.80 target (H1). External discrimination was lower yet clinically useful, and the two pandemic periods were indistinguishable (pre 0.765, 95% CI 0.724-0.804; post 0.771, 0.739-0.801; difference +0.006, 95% CI -0.045 to +0.056, computed post hoc); calibration deteriorated (slope 0.65-0.68; calibration-in-the-large [CITL] +0.51 and +1.13, under-prediction) and was restored by intercept/slope recalibration to slope 1.00 (95% CI 0.95-1.00) and 1.00 (0.97-1.00) with CITL 0.00 (H2, H3 supported). The analysis plan's parsimony margin, applied entirely inside the development data, selected a 6-item model (0.822 internal, 0.748 pre, 0.757 post; H4 supported). No leakage-controlled configuration among 2,199 catalogued runs exceeded ~0.77 externally, and the in-domain oracle reached only 0.764-0.776, whereas the same predictors reached 0.81-0.82 in equally sized Korean samples.

**Conclusions:** A Korea-developed depression screener transported to the US and across both pandemic periods with clinically useful discrimination and calibration recoverable by intercept/slope updating. The ~0.77 cross-national ceiling survived sample size, algorithm choice and covariate-shift correction, and is most consistent with a property of the transportable predictor set rather than a modeling failure. A low-burden six-item version with local recalibration is a plausible scalable digital triage aid.

**Keywords:** depression; mass screening; machine learning; external validation; transportability; COVID-19; mobile health; Republic of Korea.

## Introduction

Depression is among the leading causes of years lived with disability worldwide [1], and its detection at the community and primary-care level remains incomplete. Brief self-report instruments such as the PHQ-9 enable population-scale screening [2,3], yet applying a fixed cut-off uniformly across heterogeneous populations does not prioritize those at highest risk, even where universal screening of adults is recommended [4]. Risk-stratification tools that combine routinely available sociodemographic and behavioral information could support more efficient triage, and a tool built only from self-reportable items can be delivered as a web or mobile form and scaled at low marginal cost. National health-examination and community-health surveys already collect the sociodemographic, behavioral and clinical items such a tool would need; community-health surveillance systems in particular reach hundreds of thousands of respondents each year, which makes such surveys an attractive development base for screening algorithms intended to run at population scale.

ML models for depression have been developed repeatedly from large national surveys, including the KCHS and the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) [5,6], typically reporting strong internal discrimination. However, most published models are assessed only through internal validation [7,8]. Reviews in other clinical domains have reached the same conclusion: among hundreds of prediction models published during the COVID-19 pandemic, almost none were validated in data independent of the development source and most carried a high risk of bias [8]. A systematic review of machine-learning depression models built on electronic health records likewise found reported discrimination clustered near 0.80, with generalizability and interpretability repeatedly identified as unresolved concerns [7]. Internal performance is an optimistic indicator of real-world behavior: it does not establish that a model transports to a different sampling frame, health system, or population [9], and it does not test stability under temporal distribution shift.

The COVID-19 pandemic constitutes a natural distribution shift for mental-health prediction [10,11]. In Korea, national surveillance recorded a rise in depressive symptoms and suicide attempts over the pandemic years [12]. Population distributions of depressive symptoms and of the associated behavioral characteristics changed during the pandemic, and a screening model carried into a shifted target population may show degraded calibration even where discrimination is preserved. Whether recalibration (updating only the intercept and slope without re-estimating predictor effects [13,14]) restores clinical usefulness is a practically important, under-studied question for deployed screening tools.

This study addresses these gaps with a design that develops the model in KCHS and validates it in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), an approach well suited to the outcome. Depression is operationalized identically in both surveys (nine-item PHQ-9, 0-3 scoring, sum ≥10), minimizing outcome-harmonization error, while the surveys differ in country, health system, racial/ethnic composition, and survey mode, making NHANES a substantively external validation set. NHANES additionally provides NCHS-defined pre- and post-pandemic data, so that the same transported model can be evaluated in two periods of the same external population. Since KCHS development pools 2017-2025 and therefore spans both periods, this is a contrast of target periods rather than validation of a model frozen before the later cycle.

Two questions follow, and neither is settled by the models and reviews cited above [5-8]. The first is empirical: how much discrimination is lost when a model developed in one national survey is applied, without refitting, to another country's survey, and does that loss change across a population-wide shock such as the pandemic? The second is interpretive: when external performance falls, is the shortfall a modeling failure that better algorithms or larger training sets could recover, or a property of the transportable predictor set itself, in which case additional modeling effort cannot close it? Separating the two requires more than a single external estimate. It requires an estimate of what the same predictors can achieve inside the external population when a model is fitted there directly, and a systematic search over modeling choices in which no external label is ever used for selection.

This study pursued five aims: (1) develop an ML depression-screening model in KCHS; (2) externally validate it in US NHANES (geographic transportability); (3) evaluate it separately in the pre- and post-COVID-19 cycles and quantify recalibration (transportability across target periods); (4) derive a parsimonious model for a low-burden digital tool; and (5) determine whether any residual external shortfall is closable by modeling, using a catalogued leakage-controlled optimization registry and an in-domain oracle fitted inside the external population. The pre-specified hypotheses, which attach to aims 1 to 4, were: H1 the full model achieves internal AUROC ≥0.80; H2 external discrimination remains clinically useful while calibration may deteriorate; H3 recalibration substantially improves calibration; H4 the parsimonious model incurs only a small performance loss. No numeric hypothesis was set for aim 5, which is reported as a diagnostic analysis.

## Methods

### Study Design and Data Sources
This is a retrospective secondary-data study developing a clinical prediction model in one national survey and validating it, without refitting, in another; reporting follows Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis extended to artificial intelligence (TRIPOD+AI, 2024) [15], and risk of bias was self-assessed with the Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) [16]. The self-assessment rated the participants, predictors and outcome domains at low risk of bias in both the development and the validation data, and the analysis domain at low risk for the development data. The analysis domain was rated unclear for the external cohorts, given that depression-module non-response was substantial and differential and was handled as complete-case, which gives an overall low-to-unclear rating driven by that non-response. The study protocol and statistical analysis plan, including the four hypotheses, were written and dated before any analysis. The study was not registered in a public registry. The predictor set, including a dated protocol amendment covering three of the seventeen items, is documented below. Multimedia Appendix 1 provides the year-specific harmonization crosswalk together with an English rendition of the locked amendment; the unmodified original protocol, statistical analysis plan, and amendment document are available with the analysis code, hyperparameter grids, and model card (see Data Availability).

Development used the Korea Community Health Survey, a nationwide Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) survey [17] of community-dwelling adults that conducts annual in-person household interviews (~230,000/year) under a stratified, clustered, weighted complex-sample design. The pooled development sample was KCHS 2017-2025 records with the PHQ-9 administered (n=2,048,510). From this pooled sample, clusters defined as survey year crossed with design stratum crossed with primary sampling unit were randomly permuted and assigned whole to the hold-out until 100,001 records had accumulated; the training set is a random 500,000-record draw from the records remaining in the clusters not assigned to the hold-out. The two sides share 0 clusters, and the hold-out spans 9,636 clusters; hence the internal estimate refers to clusters the model never saw and not merely to new individuals within sampled clusters. The training-set size was fixed for computational tractability before any external evaluation, and a subsequent check confirmed that expanding it toward the full pooled sample did not improve, and slightly reduced, external discrimination.

External validation used NHANES, an NCHS survey combining household interview and mobile examination center assessment; the sample design and estimation procedures for the 2015-2018 cycles are documented by NCHS [18]; the pre-pandemic combined file and the 2021-2023 cycle carry separate NCHS documentation and weight variables, which are named in Multimedia Appendix 1; depression was measured with the Depression Screener (DPQ; PHQ-9) module. Two periods were used as separate validation sets: pre-pandemic (2017-March 2020, "P_" files; n=7,888) and post-pandemic (August 2021-August 2023, "_L" files; n=5,210).

### Participants and Outcome
Eligible respondents were adults aged 20 years or older with all nine PHQ-9 items answered. Pooling KCHS 2017-2025 gave 2,048,510 eligible records. In NHANES the depression module is administered in the mobile examination center, and the estimation frame is therefore the examined sample: of 9,232 interviewed adults aged 20 years or older in the pre-pandemic file, 8,544 were examined and 7,888 answered all nine items; the corresponding post-pandemic counts were 7,809, 6,064 and 5,210. Item-level non-response is broken down in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8. Predictors and outcome were recorded in the same survey contact and were therefore measured contemporaneously; blinding of outcome or predictor assessment does not apply to secondary analysis of already-collected survey responses. Pregnant respondents were retained in the main analysis. A prior physician diagnosis of depression is not recorded in the KCHS 2017-2025 questionnaires and therefore could not be used for exclusion or sensitivity analysis; this is noted as a limitation.

The outcome is current depression, defined as a PHQ-9 total of at least 10 and computed identically in both surveys (nine items, scored 0 to 3); the ≥10 cut-off is the conventional screening threshold and has been evaluated against semi-structured diagnostic interviews in individual-participant-data meta-analyses [3,19] and validated in a Korean population [20]. KCHS items were recoded 1-4→0-3 to match NHANES.

### Predictors and Harmonization
Only variables reducible to a common definition in both surveys entered the harmonized model; several underlying instruments differ, as set out in the harmonization cautions below. The final harmonized set comprised 17 predictors (Table 1): age, sex, education, household income (within-survey quintiles), self-rated health, partnership, living alone, smoking, alcohol, physical activity, sleep duration, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, employment (KCHS soa_01z1 ↔ NHANES OCD150), self-rated oral health (ora_01z1 ↔ OHQ845), and household food insecurity (nue_01z1 ↔ FSDAD). Concept-level harmonization used a label-based per-year crosswalk on the KCHS side and cycle-specific code branching on the NHANES side; self-rated health was harmonized to NHANES HUQ010 (correcting a file-code error in the pre-specification document, which had named HSD010/HSQ). The harmonized set is drawn from domains with established associations with depression in the epidemiological literature: socioeconomic position [21], sleep duration [22], physical activity [23], body mass index [24], smoking [25], living alone [26] and partnership status [27]. The three predictors covered by the protocol amendment are documented below.

The harmonized set was assembled by crosswalking all 616 KCHS concepts against the NHANES codebook for both cycles and retaining concepts that are (i) available in NHANES, (ii) collected in more than one KCHS survey year, and (iii) not circular with the PHQ-9 outcome. The crosswalk available when the amendment was written held a 175-variable extract, and the seventeen predictors are every eligible concept in that extract. An exhaustive extraction afterwards identified 265 variables common to both cycles; the further eligible items it surfaced are named in Multimedia Appendix 1, Part A, and tested in the next paragraph; none entered the model. The deployed set is therefore exhaustive with respect to the extract available at the time of the amendment rather than with respect to the full crosswalk. Living alone is the single retained item not present in both cycles, being absent from the pre-pandemic file. Its handling requires a caveat that the tree learner's native missing support does not remove. Living alone has no missing values anywhere in the KCHS training data; the learner therefore never observes a missing value for it and never learns a missing direction for it; at prediction time the missing values are treated as zero and follow the same branch as "not living alone." The pre-pandemic cohort is thus scored as if no respondent lived alone. Since this differs between the two NHANES cycles, the pandemic-period contrast is repeated with living alone removed from the model, and that control is reported under Sensitivity Analyses. Employment, self-rated oral health and household food insecurity entered the set through a protocol amendment that was written and dated before any model using those items was fitted; the study protocol had specified the other fourteen. The amendment is summarized in English, with contextual notes marked as such, in Multimedia Appendix 1, and the unmodified Korean original is released with the code, together with the crosswalk. Criteria (i)-(iii) use survey documentation and variable availability only, and no NHANES outcome association was estimated for any candidate before the amendment was locked. Each added predictor has an established association with depression in the epidemiological literature: food insecurity (pooled odds ratio ≈1.4) [28], unemployment (≈1.9-2.1) [29] and poor oral health (a bidirectional relationship) [30]. Circular or outcome-contaminated variables (PHQ items, suicidal ideation, EQ-5D anxiety/depression, happiness, perceived stress, mental-health service use including NHANES HUQ090) were excluded a priori.

That extraction surfaced five further harmonizable items. Each was screened after the primary analysis by comparing the direction of its adjusted association with depression across the three cohorts and then testing it in the model: three reversed direction between countries and the remainder added no external discrimination (Multimedia Appendix 1, Part A). Since this screening inspected NHANES outcome associations, it is reported as a post-hoc diagnostic; no item was added, and the predictor set is therefore unchanged. 

Three harmonization cautions attach to the set. A direct Korea-US comparison using the same 18-item household module found food insecurity associated with higher odds of PHQ-9 depression in both countries, with a larger association in Korea [31]; the single-item KCHS measure used here is therefore the weakest link in the harmonized set rather than a difference in the underlying construct. Alcohol, physical activity, and income use different instruments and were reduced to conservatively comparable definitions. Only the alcohol definition could be varied in sensitivity analysis; the harmonized data carry a single binary physical-activity indicator and a within-survey income quintile; hence no alternative definition of those two was available. The protocol specified alcohol as current drinking plus at-risk drinking, whereas the harmonized variable uses current drinking alone, given that the NHANES quantity item was not carried through harmonization; this is recorded as a definition-level deviation (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S9). BMI is self-reported in KCHS, measured in NHANES. Two further scale differences arise from NHANES top-coding: age is capped at 80 years in NHANES, whereas 3.8% of the weighted KCHS sample is older (up to 110); the same cap was therefore applied to KCHS before fitting, so that the model cannot separate respondents above 80 in either survey and that part of the Korean age range is compressed; and NHANES sleep duration is bounded at 2-14 hours while KCHS admits 0-23, though implausible KCHS values account for only 0.01% of respondents. Three further discontinuities are internal to KCHS: the current-drinking items were recoded in 2020, and the weighted prevalence rises from 72.5-75.4% before that year to 81.8-86.8% after it; the 2018 physical-activity module lacks the moderate-activity day count; and sleep duration is a weekday-only figure in 2017-2019 but a weekday-weighted seven-day average from 2020, whereas NHANES asks about weekdays. These three discontinuities, together with the age top-code, the 2017 income refusal codes and the income quintile, were corrected in the analytic data before the primary analysis rather than carried as limitations; the corrections are itemized in the data-integrity audit reported below and quantified singly in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S10.

All missing-data handling was performed by a preprocessing pipeline fitted in the development training set and applied unchanged to the internal hold-out and to each external set (no leakage). The household income quintile is the one transformation not carried over unchanged: it is a within-cohort rank score recomputed in each dataset and, inside KCHS, within each survey year, since the underlying income instruments are not on a common scale. In the gradient-boosting primary model, binary predictors were passed through with missingness preserved and handled natively by the tree learner, while continuous predictors were median-imputed and categorical predictors mode-imputed within the same pipeline. For the linear comparator, continuous predictors were median-imputed, categorical predictors mode-imputed, and binary predictors mode-imputed with an added missing indicator. Household income (within-survey quintile), self-rated health and self-rated oral health are ordinal scores and were modeled as numeric variables rather than as unordered categories, and were median-imputed and standardized. In the post-pandemic cohort the income-to-poverty ratio is top-coded at 5.00 for 23.8% of respondents; hence the income quintile would collapse to four levels if the raw ratio were binned directly; the primary analysis instead recomputes a rank-based five-level score within each cohort, which restores five levels. Alternative strategies (missing indicators added for the continuous predictors; single chained-equations imputation of the continuous predictors) were examined in sensitivity analysis. A check for silently omitted predictor columns was added to the pipeline after the data-integrity audit reported below.

### Model Development and Evaluation
No a priori sample-size calculation was performed; the samples are those the data allow. Development draws a 500,000-record training set and a 100,001-record hold-out from the 2,048,510 eligible pooled KCHS records, and each external cohort is every eligible respondent in that cycle. What that supplies is reported instead: the training set carries approximately 18,000 events, about 1,000 per candidate predictor and far above conventional events-per-predictor requirements, and the hold-out approximately 3,600. The external cohorts (728 events pre, 661 post; weighted prevalence 8.4% and 12.2%) are the binding constraint on precision and govern external calibration and decision-curve precision; hence discrimination is reported with confidence intervals rather than as point estimates alone, and subgroup estimates are presented with intervals and read as exploratory. Interval construction is set out below.

Three algorithms were considered: penalized logistic regression (elastic net), random forest, and gradient boosting (LightGBM). Development proceeded in two selection steps, both confined to the KCHS training set. First, the gradient-boosting hyperparameters and the feature representation were chosen by a randomized search over 24 LightGBM configurations crossed with two representations: main effects only versus main effects plus four candidate interaction terms (age×self-rated health, self-rated health×oral health, age×employment, income×employment; specified when the search was written but not named in any earlier document). The comparison used 4-fold survey-weighted cross-validation in a 300,000-record subsample of the training set; the main-effects-only representation won, and adding interaction terms did not improve cross-validated discrimination. Algorithm and hyperparameter selection used individual-level cross-validation within the training set; the cluster-level separation applies to the hold-out and to the parsimony selection set; the selection step may therefore be mildly optimistic while the reported performance is not. Second, all three algorithms were tuned over comparable 5-configuration grids under an identical protocol (5-fold stratified cross-validation in a 200,000-record subsample of the training set, scored by unweighted cross-validated AUROC) and compared at each algorithm's best configuration; comparing a penalized linear model against two tree ensembles under one protocol follows evidence that machine learning shows no systematic advantage over regression once validation bias is controlled [32]. That protocol equalizes the final comparison but not the whole development history, since only gradient boosting received the preceding 24-configuration search; the comparison is therefore reported as a like-for-like contest at each algorithm's tuned best rather than as equal cumulative effort.

The analysis plan fixed the primary-model rule in advance: highest cross-validated AUROC, with differences smaller than 0.005 treated as ties and broken by proximity of the calibration slope to 1, and only then by parsimony. The pipeline as executed ranked the algorithms directly on AUROC and then calibration slope without constructing the tie band; the plan's rule was therefore checked retrospectively.

No NHANES data were used in fitting, tuning or selecting the primary model. The in-domain oracle reported under Testing Whether External Discrimination Can Be Raised Above ~0.77 is fitted on NHANES labels by design, as a diagnostic upper bound rather than as a candidate model. The predictor set is the one exception: three of the seventeen items entered through a dated amendment written after the external result for the earlier set was known, as set out under Predictors and Harmonization. Complex-survey weights were handled by unweighted training with weighted evaluation (probed in sensitivity analysis). No class weighting, resampling, or other imbalance correction was applied; the learner was fitted on the natural 3.6% event rate, which departs from the plan's recommended class weighting (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S9). The plan also asked for 5-fold cross-validation repeated five times; the searches were run single-pass, which is a departure recorded in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S9, and one that matters here, since the margin between the two leading algorithms is smaller than the tie band. LightGBM (0.840) and random forest (0.835) differ by 0.0046 and therefore fall inside the tie band, where the decision passes to the calibration slope; the two slopes differ by 0.20 (1.003 for LightGBM against 1.203 for random forest), which is not a close call, and the tie-break selects LightGBM. Elastic net (0.822) lies outside the band, and the parsimony criterion is never reached. The retrospective check therefore reproduces the model the pipeline selected (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S9). LightGBM was refitted on the full 500,000-record training set; the selected configuration was num_leaves 31, min_child_samples 1000, learning_rate 0.01, n_estimators 900, reg_alpha 1.0, reg_lambda 20, colsample_bytree 0.6, and subsample 0.6 with subsample_freq at its default of 0; the full search grids are specified in the released analysis code, and the selected configuration is recorded in the model card (see Data Availability). The selected row-subsampling fraction is inactive in the deployed configuration, given that the subsampling frequency was left at its default of zero; the fitted model therefore uses all rows at every boosting iteration.

Performance was summarized by discrimination (AUROC and area under the precision-recall curve [AUPRC]), calibration, and operating characteristics, all complex-survey weighted. Calibration was assessed at the moderate level of the calibration hierarchy [33] and summarized by the calibration slope and by calibration-in-the-large (CITL), the latter estimated as the intercept of a weighted logistic model with the linear predictor entered as an offset (ie, with the slope fixed at 1), together with the Brier score and expected calibration error (ECE); a positive CITL indicates that observed risk exceeds predicted risk. Ninety-five percent CIs are reported for AUROC and AUPRC and, in the two external cohorts, for the calibration slope and CITL both as transported and after recalibration. ECE is reported as a point estimate only, for the reason given with Table S16. For the recalibrated columns the interval is obtained by refitting the intercept and slope inside every replicate, so that the uncertainty of the update itself is carried; the wider fixed-update alternative is tabulated alongside it in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S16. The difference in external AUROC between the two cycles is also given an interval. Since the two cycles are disjoint samples, that interval is formed by differencing independently drawn replicate statistics rather than by pairing replicate indices. Operating characteristics and net benefit are point estimates. Bootstrap P values are two-sided and are read off the replicate distribution as twice the smaller tail proportion; with 1000 replicates the smallest attainable value is reported as P<.001 rather than as an equality. CIs were obtained by a rescaled stratified cluster bootstrap with 1000 replicates, intervals taken as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the replicate distribution, for cohort-level and subgroup estimates alike: within each design stratum, *n*ₕ-1 primary sampling units were resampled with replacement and the survey weights rescaled by *n*ₕ/(*n*ₕ-1) times the selection count [34]. Rescaling is necessary given that most NHANES strata contain only two primary sampling units, for which an ordinary within-stratum cluster bootstrap understates variance. The implementation was verified against a closed-form design-based (linearized ultimate-cluster) standard error for weighted depression prevalence: bootstrap 0.00466 vs design 0.00471 in the pre-pandemic cycle and 0.00802 vs 0.00796 in the post-pandemic cycle, ie, agreement to within about 1.1%. For KCHS the stratum is defined as survey year crossed with the design stratum, owing to weight normalization within year and to the recurrence of stratum codes across years. Subgroup intervals are domain estimates: replicate weights are generated once on the full cohort design and then applied to each subgroup, rather than rebuilding the design after subsetting. Strata left with a single primary sampling unit are pooled into one pseudo-stratum, ensuring that the variance contribution of those strata is retained. A single operating threshold was fixed once in the development data at the predicted probability giving 85% sensitivity (0.02051) and then applied unchanged to the internal hold-out and to both external cohorts, so that the external operating characteristics are transported values rather than values selected in the cohort where those characteristics are reported. Clinical utility was assessed by weighted decision-curve analysis [35] over threshold probabilities of 0.02 to 0.30, each dataset carrying its own screen-all and screen-none default strategies.

### External Validation and Sensitivity Analyses
Geographic transportability was assessed by applying the KCHS-trained model to NHANES without refitting. Transportability across target periods was assessed by evaluating that same transported model separately in the pre- and post-pandemic cycles; since KCHS development pools 2017-2025 and spans both periods, this contrasts target periods rather than validating a model frozen before the later cycle. Recovery of calibration was then quantified by intercept and slope updating applied out-of-sample, which corrects calibration without re-estimating any predictor effect [13,14]. Updating the intercept and slope rather than refitting is the conventional first step when a model is moved to a new population [36]. Following the plan, recalibration was applied in three steps, each cross-fitted over five folds grouped by primary sampling unit, so that no cluster contributes both to fitting an update and to evaluating it: intercept only, intercept plus slope (the two-parameter Platt-type update), and one-parameter temperature scaling with the intercept fixed at zero. Table 2 tabulates the intercept-plus-slope result, which is the recommended update; the other two steps are reported numerically in the Results.

Three further analyses were undertaken: interpretability, through global importance from tree-based Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) [37] and cross-survey rank concordance of predictor importance (Spearman correlation between KCHS and each NHANES cycle) as a mechanistic view of transportability; fairness, through weighted subgroup AUROC and calibration slope across sex, age, education, and (NHANES) race/ethnicity, since algorithmic performance can differ systematically across groups even when overall accuracy is high [38]; and a parsimonious model for a low-burden self-administered form.

For the parsimonious model, both the predictor order and the item count were determined entirely inside the development training set, which was split into a 450,000-record fitting set and a 50,000-record selection set: predictors were entered in descending order of global SHAP importance computed on a random 20,000-record subsample of the fitting set, the model was refitted at every item count from 1 to 17, and the item count was chosen on selection-set AUROC. The item count follows the analysis plan's margin (the smallest set within 0.02 AUROC of the full model). The search ran over every count below 17 and did not impose the plan's preferred 5-10 item range, which the selected count nonetheless satisfies; a variant using a stricter 0.01 margin is reported alongside it and flagged as post hoc. The internal hold-out and both NHANES cohorts were used only for evaluation.

The model is intended as a triage aid for population-level screening programs and web or mobile self-assessment services, to be operated by public-health and primary-care services rather than by individuals acting alone, and to prioritize who receives a full PHQ-9 and clinical follow-up. It is not a diagnostic instrument and does not replace clinical assessment. Deployment presupposes local recalibration. It also presupposes a reference distribution: the household income predictor is a within-cohort rank; hence the 17-predictor model cannot be scored from one respondent's answers alone and needs a local income distribution to rank against. The six-item form carries no such variable and no measured value; every one of its items is self-reportable in a web or mobile form, which is what makes it, rather than the full model, the version suited to remote and mobile delivery.

Sensitivity analyses were pre-specified, with further analyses added afterwards: the outcome cut-off (≥5 as well as ≥10), removal of the three added predictors (→14) and of food insecurity alone (→16), weighted versus unweighted training, two alternative imputation schemes, an age lower bound of 30 years applied to training *and* evaluation, a protocol-specified continuous-score analysis of how the model ranks the untransformed PHQ-9 total, and three alternative alcohol definitions. Weighted AUROC is tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S5 for every specification apart from the continuous-score and alcohol analyses, which are reported in the Results text. The self-report (KCHS) versus measured (NHANES) difference in BMI is inherent to the data sources and is addressed in the harmonization cautions under Predictors and Harmonization and under Strengths and Limitations rather than by swapping measurements.

All analyses were performed in Python 3.14, with package versions listed in the model card; complex-survey weighting, the stratified cluster bootstrap, calibration measures, and decision-curve net benefit were implemented directly rather than through a survey-analysis package, and the implementations are released with the code. Intervals are not reported for net benefit, since inference on a decision-analytic quantity answers a different question from the decision itself [39].

An adversarial data-integrity audit corrected three silent data-processing defects before final analysis: living alone fabricated to a constant in NHANES pre-pandemic, post-pandemic physical activity coded 0 for every respondent through a unit-field parsing error, and an income refusal code (77777) not treated as missing, which had placed non-respondents in the top income quintile. Peer review identified four further defects, all corrected in the primary analysis rather than carried forward as limitations: the income quintile collapsed to four levels post-pandemic through top-coding of the income-to-poverty ratio; the 2017 income refusal codes entered the highest quintile; post-2020 annual non-drinkers were coded as current drinkers (17.9% weighted of 2020-2025); and the 2018 physical-activity indicator used a different construct from adjacent years. The remedies were: post-2020 annual non-drinkers recoded as non-drinkers; the 2018 activity indicator set to missing rather than silently redefined; the 2017 refusal codes masked and the income quintile recomputed as a rank-based five-level score within each survey year, which repairs both the four-level collapse and the scale mismatch between the 2017 ordinal field and the later monetary fields; sleep duration expressed on the weekday basis NHANES uses in every KCHS year; the age top-code aligned at 80 years in all cohorts; and the internal split rebuilt at cluster level. Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S10 quantifies, one at a time, six of these corrections: the three questionnaire-driven KCHS discontinuities above (the 2020 drinking recode, the 2018 activity fallback and the sleep alignment), the age alignment, the 2017 KCHS income refusal codes, and the income-quintile rebuild, the last of which also removes the four-level collapse in the post-pandemic cohort. Three are not tabulated separately: the living-alone and physical-activity audit corrections, since in the uncorrected data the affected variable is constant rather than mismeasured, and the KCHS 77777 income refusal code, which is a missing-value declaration rather than a change of definition.

## Results

### Participant Characteristics
The pooled KCHS development sample comprised 2,048,510 adults and the external cohorts 7,888 pre-pandemic and 5,210 post-pandemic NHANES adults, similar in mean age (49.4, 48.4 and 48.8 years) and in sex composition (50.5%, 51.5% and 50.8% female) but not in outcome frequency. Weighted depression prevalence (PHQ-9 ≥10) was 3.6% in KCHS against 8.4% and 12.2% in the two NHANES cycles (Figure 1). The surveys also differed markedly on measured covariates: mean BMI 23.7 (KCHS) vs 29.8 and 29.7 (NHANES pre and post), and weighted food-insecurity prevalence 3.7% in KCHS vs 16.4% and 17.2% in the NHANES cycles, computed among respondents with the item recorded (missing 22.1%, 6.0% and 6.3%, respectively; every characteristic in Table S4 uses available-case denominators). This pattern is consistent with genuinely distinct populations and, for food insecurity, with the single-item-vs-module measurement difference.

Depression-module non-response was appreciable in both NHANES cycles and larger after the pandemic. The relevant denominator is the mobile examination center (MEC) frame, since the depression module is administered there and every weighted estimate in this study uses MEC weights: of 8,544 pre-pandemic and 6,064 post-pandemic MEC-examined adults ≥20 years, 7.7% and 14.1%, respectively, were lost to PHQ-9 item non-response, yielding the complete-case analytic samples of 7,888 and 5,210 (item-level detail in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8). The corresponding three-or-more-item rates are 7.4% and 13.3% on the MEC frame, against 14.3% and 32.7% when counted over all interviewed adults; that second denominator conflates module non-response with never having been examined, which the MEC weights already account for. Non-response was differential: higher among respondents with less than high-school education (12.9% pre, 26.6% post, MEC frame) and among Asian and Black respondents; hence the external cohorts, and particularly the post-pandemic fairness estimates, should be read as complete-case results. Reassuringly, adding respondents missing only one or two items, with the missing items filled by the person-mean, changed the observed prevalence by ≤0.1 percentage points in both cycles (unweighted: 9.23%→9.27% pre, 12.69%→12.78% post). Exact values are tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8.

### Model Development and Internal Performance
Under the identical tuning protocol, cross-validated AUROC in the training set was 0.840 for LightGBM, 0.835 for random forest and 0.822 for elastic net; the two leading algorithms fell inside the analysis plan's 0.005 tie band, which the calibration-slope tie-break resolves in favor of LightGBM (cross-validated slope 1.00 against 1.20), while elastic net lay outside it (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S9). LightGBM was therefore the primary model, and the plan's tie band, reconstructed after the fact, reproduces the choice the pipeline made. Internal hold-out AUROC was 0.836 (95% CI 0.827-0.846), AUPRC 0.246, Brier 0.030, calibration slope 1.03, CITL +0.10; H1 met. Given that the split is at cluster level, this is an estimate for clusters the model never saw.

### External Validation Across Country and Pandemic Period
External discrimination fell by about 0.07 AUROC from the internal estimate and differed little between the two cycles: pre 0.765 (95% CI 0.724-0.804), post 0.771 (0.739-0.801) (Table 2; Figures 2 and 3); the difference is +0.006 (95% CI -0.045 to +0.056; bootstrap P=.86), an interval computed after the fact, since no between-cycle test was pre-specified; the data are therefore consistent with no deterioration across the pandemic but cannot exclude a modest change. AUPRC moved in the opposite direction (0.246 internally, 0.262 pre and 0.345 post), which follows from the higher external event rate rather than from better external ranking: precision-recall summaries rise with prevalence, and each value should be read against its own no-skill baseline of 3.6%, 8.4% and 12.2%. Calibration degraded in both cycles: the slope fell to 0.65 (pre) and 0.68 (post) and CITL rose to +0.51 and +1.13, indicating that the Korea-trained model systematically *under*-predicted absolute risk in the US, as expected from the prevalence difference (KCHS 3.6% vs 8.4% and 12.2%). Of the three pre-specified recalibration steps applied out-of-sample, intercept-only updating removed the mean bias but left the slope unchanged (0.65 pre, 0.68 post), intercept-plus-slope updating restored both (slope 1.00 and 1.00, CITL 0.00), and temperature scaling was intermediate, leaving the slope at 0.83 pre and 1.17 post with CITL +0.02 and -0.02; intercept-plus-slope is therefore the recommended deployment update; H2 and H3 supported.

### Testing Whether External Discrimination Can Be Raised Above ~0.77
Leakage-controlled optimization (2,199 recorded runs across 117 experiment files, catalogued in the released registry: feature engineering, hyperparameter search, heterogeneous ensembling, transfer-robust internal selection, domain adaptation, alternative losses, and structural variants) did not exceed external AUROC ~0.77 when model selection was restricted to KCHS-internal criteria (Multimedia Appendix 1, Tables S11 and S12). Apparent gains from externally tuned variants (eg, specific interaction terms, ensemble weights) did not survive leakage control.

The ceiling was localized with an in-domain oracle: fitting NHANES on its own labels, a deliberate departure from the external-validation rule, made for diagnosis only. Across logistic, penalized-logistic, histogram-gradient-boosting and LightGBM learners, the oracle was cross-validated with folds split by primary sampling unit within each cycle so that no cluster appeared in both training and validation, and reached only 0.764-0.776 (best learner 0.776, 95% CI 0.753-0.799; widening to 0.745-0.794 when the oracle is refitted inside each bootstrap replicate so that model-fitting variability is included; Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S15). The KCHS-transferred model (pre 0.765, post 0.771) therefore sits inside the confidence interval of the best in-domain performance any of these learners achieved when fitted directly to NHANES. This is an empirical benchmark over the learners evaluated, not a proof of a maximum; it is estimated on the pooled pre+post sample whereas the transported figures are cycle-specific, and its interval overlaps the transported estimates; thus the comparison is approximate and cannot exclude a modest true gap. Matching sample size does not close the gap: reducing the Korean data to the NHANES sample size and cross-validating within it still yielded 0.823 at n=7,888 (the pre-pandemic cohort size) and 0.810 at n=13,098 (the pooled oracle size), rising to 0.830 only at n=200,000 (best of three learners, 5-fold weighted AUROC, in a separate KCHS-internal sample-size experiment). At identical n, in other words, the same 17 predictors carry 0.81-0.82 of discrimination in Korea against an NHANES ceiling of ~0.77.

Three further checks converged on the same conclusion: (i) covariate-shift correction by density-ratio reweighting of the KCHS training sample toward the NHANES covariate distribution (labels unused) *reduced* external AUROC, to 0.766 and 0.758 against the baseline of the exploration run that anchors Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S11 (0.772 pre, 0.769 post), showing that this particular correction did not help, which weakens but does not exclude a covariate-shift explanation; (ii) increasing the KCHS training set from 0.5 million to 2 million records did not raise, and slightly lowered, external discrimination, consistent with source-specific overfitting rather than an estimation-limited ceiling; and (iii) adding the remaining harmonized-but-unused survey items (public-assistance receipt, weekend sleep duration, alcohol frequency, alcohol quantity in two codings and binge drinking) changed external AUROC by at most 0.003 in either direction, a check evaluated on external data and therefore reported as a diagnostic rather than as leakage-controlled evidence. These are changes against the in-run baseline of that same exploration run and are not comparable in level with the primary estimates. Deep tabular learners (two multilayer perceptrons, TabNet, and an FT-Transformer) were additionally trained and transferred; all fell below gradient boosting internally and none exceeded it on both external cohorts (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S13), consistent with the model-agnostic ceiling.

Sample size, model choice, covariate imbalance and omitted harmonized information were each tested and none closed the gap, and the gap is not closable by recalibration either, which corrects calibration rather than rank ordering. The most consistent remaining explanation is shift in the predictor-outcome relationship itself (concept shift), which these data cannot separate from the surveys' differing predictor measurement and from complete-case selection; the interpretation is developed in the Discussion.

### Parsimonious Deployment Model
Predictor order and item count were both fixed inside the training set, and the analysis plan's margin (the smallest set within 0.02 AUROC of the full model) selected six items: self-rated health, age, sex, sleep duration, self-rated oral health, partnership. Evaluated afterwards on data that played no part in selection, this six-item form achieved hold-out AUROC 0.822, NHANES pre 0.748 and NHANES post 0.757, against 0.837/0.766/0.769 for the full 17-predictor model fitted on the same data (a loss of 0.015 internally, 0.018 pre-pandemic and 0.012 post-pandemic, each within the plan's 0.02 tolerance); H4 supported. Utility followed the same pattern: at a within-cohort operating point fixed to 85% sensitivity (apparent values, Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S2), the six-item form required 7.9 (pre) and 5.7 (post) respondents screened per case detected against 7.7 and 5.3 for the 17-predictor model, and its net benefit exceeded both default strategies in every cohort at threshold probabilities of 0.10 and 0.15 (Table S3). On the selection set, discrimination rose steeply and then flattened (1 item 0.741; 3 items 0.781; 5 items 0.813; 6 items 0.818; 8 items 0.825; 12 items 0.832; 17 items 0.833), the margin being applied over every count below 17 without imposing the plan's preferred 5-10 item range. The five-item set falls 0.0002 below the threshold and the six-item set clears it by 0.0044; the five-versus-six boundary is therefore decided at the fourth decimal and should not be read as robust, and the five-item model reaches 0.737 pre-pandemic and 0.751 post-pandemic externally. The stricter-margin seven-item variant is reported only as an exploratory comparison; no external information entered the item-count decision itself. The full k=1-17 trajectory is tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S6.

### Sensitivity Analyses
Discrimination was stable across specifications that alter modeling choices rather than the target condition (Figure 4): weighted training (0.836/0.764/0.768 internal/pre/post), missing-indicator imputation (0.837/0.764/0.772), single chained-equations imputation of the continuous predictors (0.837/0.763/0.770), and predictor-set variants (below) all lay close to the primary model internally. Restricting both training and evaluation to respondents aged ≥30 years *improved* discrimination (0.838/0.781/0.787), consistent with the lower subgroup performance observed at ages 20-39; in an additional variant restricting training alone, discrimination was 0.836/0.770/0.769, which places most of that improvement in the evaluation case mix rather than in what the model learned. Changing the outcome definition to PHQ-9 ≥5 lowered discrimination throughout (0.761/0.735/0.740) at a much higher weighted prevalence (15.6%/25.0%/32.1%), as expected when the target shifts from moderate-or-worse symptoms to mild or greater symptom burden; notably, the Korea-US gap narrows from about 0.07 AUROC at the ≥10 threshold to about 0.02 at ≥5 (0.761 vs 0.735-0.740), which is consistent with, but does not establish, a larger cross-national difference at the more severe end of the symptom distribution. Replacing current drinking with drinking at least twice weekly, with binge drinking, or with all three indicators together (the alternative alcohol definitions listed in the analysis plan) changed discrimination by at most 0.003 externally, the alternatives reaching 0.769-0.771 pre-pandemic and 0.770 post-pandemic against an in-run baseline of 0.772 and 0.769; that run predates the data-integrity corrections and the cluster-level split, hence its levels are not comparable with the primary estimates or with Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S5. Each harmonization correction was additionally assessed singly against the uncorrected baseline, in which the other corrections are not yet applied (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S10); no single tabulated correction, the age top-code alignment and the rank-based income-quintile rebuild included, changed weighted AUROC by more than 0.003 in any cohort.

The harmonized set is also robust to its least-established members: refitting without employment, self-rated oral health and food insecurity (the three items whose inclusion is documented in the protocol amendment) lowers external discrimination to 0.745 (pre) and 0.757 (post) when each model is given its own hyperparameter search, a difference of Δ+0.020, 95% CI +0.010 to +0.030 and Δ+0.014, 95% CI +0.006 to +0.020 (P<.001 for both; paired rescaled cluster bootstrap). Under the common configuration of Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S5, where the 17-, 16- and 14-predictor models share one setting and the 14-predictor value is 0.758 post-pandemic, removing food insecurity alone accounts for 28% of the 17-versus-14 gap pre-pandemic and 50% post-pandemic. In the pre-specified continuous-score analysis, run in the same pre-correction generation as the alcohol analysis and on a record-level rather than cluster-level split, a classifier of the deployed specification ranked the untransformed PHQ-9 total with weighted Spearman ρ=0.378 internally, 0.403 pre-pandemic and 0.439 post-pandemic, and a model trained directly on the continuous score performed similarly (ρ=0.402/0.403/0.424; weighted R² 0.216/0.166/0.102); thus the transportability pattern is not an artifact of dichotomizing the outcome. Data-integrity fixes did not alter substantive conclusions. Exact values for the specification analyses are tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S5, and the effect of each harmonization correction applied singly in Table S10.

Living alone is recorded in every KCHS year and in the post-pandemic NHANES cycle but is absent from the pre-pandemic file, and it has no missing values in the training data, which leaves the learner routing the missing pre-pandemic column to the same branch as "not living alone" (Predictors and Harmonization). The pandemic-period contrast therefore mixes a period difference with a predictor-availability difference. Refitting the model on the 16 predictors observed in both cycles removes that difference and leaves the contrast unchanged: external AUROC 0.765 pre-pandemic and 0.771 post-pandemic, a difference of +0.006 (95% CI -0.044 to +0.056) against +0.006 (-0.045 to +0.056) for the 17-predictor model, with internal discrimination 0.836 against 0.836. The availability difference therefore does not account for the cross-cycle result.

Response-propensity weighting gave the same answer: reweighting each external cohort by the inverse of its education-specific depression-module response rate moved external AUROC by at most 0.002 (0.765 vs 0.765 pre-pandemic and 0.769 vs 0.771 post-pandemic); differential non-response by education therefore does not by itself explain the external level.

### Interpretability, Fairness, and Clinical Utility
Global SHAP importance, computed in the development fitting partition, ranked the predictors: self-rated health (0.59); age (0.37); sex (0.28); sleep duration (0.26); self-rated oral health (0.23); partnership (0.19); smoking (0.17). Food insecurity contributed modestly (0.06, ranked 10th). The predictors carrying most of the signal are therefore the general-health, demographic and sleep items that both surveys measure identically (Figure 5). The rank ordering of predictor importance was highly concordant between KCHS and both NHANES cycles (Spearman ρ=0.97 pre, 0.99 post), indicating that the transported model draws on the predictors in a similar order in both populations. This is a statement about how the fixed model attributes risk, not about the external predictor-outcome associations, since the attribution uses no external outcome; it therefore constrains, without establishing, the interpretation that the limit lies in the strength rather than the structure of those associations. The clearest exception was food insecurity, which ranked 10th in KCHS but 8th in NHANES pre and on which the transported model leaned substantially harder externally (mean absolute SHAP 0.06 in KCHS against 0.18 and 0.15 in the NHANES cycles), consistent with its different measurement (single item vs 10-item module) and much higher prevalence in the US.

Subgroup discrimination in KCHS spanned 0.781-0.882 (weighted AUROC): sex (female 0.826, 95% CI 0.814-0.837; male 0.838, 0.822-0.854), education (0.811-0.882), age (20-39 years 0.808, 0.790-0.827 < 40-59 years 0.822, 0.801-0.842 < ≥60 years 0.860, 0.850-0.870), and household income quintile (0.781-0.835, lowest in the highest-income quintile). In NHANES, discrimination was lower and far less precisely estimated. Pre-pandemic: Mexican American 0.693 (0.633-0.763; n=914), non-Hispanic Black 0.762 (0.727-0.805; n=2,096), other/multiracial 0.764 (0.665-0.865; n=381), non-Hispanic White 0.769 (0.715-0.818; n=2,798), other Hispanic 0.773 (0.725-0.820; n=813), non-Hispanic Asian 0.807 (0.716-0.874; n=886). Post-pandemic: Mexican American 0.692 (0.533-0.802; n=330), non-Hispanic Black 0.726 (0.642-0.791; n=598), non-Hispanic White 0.773 (0.726-0.815; n=3,215), other/multiracial 0.782 (0.701-0.853; n=340), other Hispanic 0.789 (0.751-0.845; n=499), non-Hispanic Asian 0.845 (0.656-0.987; n=228). Applying the fairness flag set in the analysis plan (a subgroup AUROC differing from the cohort value by more than 0.05) flags the highest income quintile in KCHS (-0.056); Mexican American in NHANES pre-pandemic (-0.072); Mexican American in NHANES post-pandemic (-0.078); non-Hispanic Asian in NHANES post-pandemic (+0.074). Within KCHS a few intervals sit clear of the cohort interval: higher in adults aged ≥60 years (0.850-0.870), middle-school-educated respondents (0.861-0.902); lower in income quintile 4 (0.772-0.822), the highest income quintile (0.744-0.818). Subgroup discrimination is therefore not uniform in the development population. In NHANES, by contrast, the intervals are wide and mutually overlapping, and every one overlaps the cohort-level interval; hence the race/ethnicity ordering should not be read as established differential performance; that the lowest point estimate falls in Mexican American respondents in both cycles nonetheless warrants monitoring as an equity caveat (Figure 6). Internal subgroup calibration was good (slope 0.96-1.13 across sex, age, education and income strata; subgroup CITL -0.03 to +0.20). In NHANES the subgroup calibration slopes were below 1 in all but one subgroup (0.57-0.79; non-Hispanic Asian post-pandemic 1.19), and subgroup CITL was positive in every group except Mexican American pre-pandemic (up to +1.32), confirming that the external miscalibration is under-prediction of absolute risk and not only over-extreme ranking. Exact subgroup values with CIs are tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S7. 

On decision-curve analysis (weighted, with cohort-specific screen-all and screen-none default strategies), the model provided positive net benefit exceeding both defaults across a clinically relevant threshold-probability range in all three datasets (Figure 7). At a threshold probability of 0.10, net benefit was +0.028 pre-pandemic and +0.058 post-pandemic, the external values computed after intercept/slope updating, against +0.010 internally, in each case above screen-all (-0.017, +0.024 and -0.072) and above screen-none (zero by definition). The operating threshold was fixed once in the development data at the point giving 85% sensitivity and then applied unchanged to the hold-out and to both external cohorts; hence the external operating characteristics are transported values rather than values guaranteed by construction. At that single threshold, sensitivity/specificity were 0.833/0.665 internally, 0.810/0.566 in NHANES pre and 0.791/0.596 in NHANES post, with positive predictive value 0.147 and 0.214 externally (number needed to screen 6.8 and 4.7) and negative predictive value 0.970 and 0.953; the model flagged 47% of the pre-pandemic and 45% of the post-pandemic population. The flagged share is a function of where the threshold is set rather than a fixed property of the model: at the decision-curve thresholds of 0.10 and 0.15 it falls to 27% and 16% pre-pandemic and to 40% and 26% post-pandemic (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S3). Full operating characteristics and net benefit are in Multimedia Appendix 1, Tables S1, S2 and S3. Clinical utility remains provisional pending prospective evaluation.

## Discussion

### Principal Findings
A depression-screening ML model developed in Korea and externally validated in the US met its internal target (AUROC 0.836) and retained clinically useful, though lower, discrimination (~0.77) on genuinely external US data and across the COVID-19 shift, with calibration recoverable by lightweight recalibration. Further modeling did not raise external discrimination: the plateau is most consistent with shift in how the same predictors relate to the outcome, with residual contributions from differing measurement, development-survey coverage and complete-case selection, and is not attributable to a deficiency of the algorithm.

Three findings carry the main argument, and a fourth, developed below, bounds it. First, external discrimination was 0.765 (95% CI 0.724-0.804) before the pandemic and 0.771 (0.739-0.801) after it; the difference is +0.006 (95% CI -0.045 to +0.056; bootstrap P=.86); the two cycles are therefore not distinguishable at this sample size. That interval was computed after the fact, no between-cycle test having been pre-specified, and its width leaves moderate change in either direction admissible; within that limit the data are consistent with no decay across the population-wide shock that motivated the analysis. Second, calibration behaved as transportability theory predicts: the slope fell to 0.65 and 0.68 and calibration-in-the-large rose to +0.51 and +1.13, meaning the Korea-trained model systematically under-predicted absolute risk in a higher-prevalence population, and intercept-plus-slope updating restored both without re-estimating any predictor effect, to slope 1.00 (0.95-1.00) and 1.00 (0.97-1.00), with the update refitted inside every replicate so that its own uncertainty is carried. Third, a six-item form selected entirely inside the development data lost only 0.015 internally and 0.018 and 0.012 externally, which places a low-burden self-report instrument within reach for digital triage. Whether triage delivered this way improves depression outcomes was not evaluated here; the mobile-delivery evidence base concerns intervention delivery to defined patient groups [40] rather than the delivery of screening, and no equivalent trial evidence exists for a transported screening algorithm.

Operating characteristics support the usefulness claim, which rests on a decision-analytic criterion rather than on a nominal AUROC cut-off. With the threshold fixed once in the development data at 85% sensitivity and then applied unchanged, external sensitivity was 0.810 and 0.791 with a number needed to screen of 6.8 and 4.7, and decision-curve net benefit exceeded both screen-all and screen-none defaults in every dataset, with the external cohorts taken after local recalibration.

### Comparison With Prior Work
Prior ML depression models built on Korean national surveys report strong internal performance but no external validation. Using KNHANES 2014-2018 restricted to non-smokers at risk of secondhand smoke exposure (n=11,463), Kim et al [6] classified PHQ-9 ≥10 with internal AUROC up to 0.90; using the 2023 KCHS restricted to culturally diverse families (n=2,568), Kim et al [5] predicted the continuous PHQ-9 score with a test R² of 33%. Both studies identified perceived stress and subjective health as dominant predictors, and neither attempted external validation. Two deliberate design choices plausibly account for the lower internal discrimination (0.836), although no ablation was run to attribute the difference quantitatively across studies that differ in population and outcome scale: outcome-proximal predictors such as perceived stress and single-item depressed-mood questions (which inflate apparent performance but are near-tautological with the outcome) were excluded; and the predictor set was restricted to variables transportable to a second country. The wider field supplies the same contrast in aggregate: a systematic review of machine-learning depression models built on electronic health records found reported discrimination clustered near 0.80, almost always internal [7], and among hundreds of prediction models published during the pandemic almost none were validated outside the development source [8]. An internal 0.836 is therefore unremarkable against that literature; an externally validated 0.77 in a second country has few published counterparts.

In exchange for those two design choices, this study provides what prior work did not: cross-national and cross-pandemic external validation, an explicit transportability ceiling characterized by an in-domain oracle and a catalogued set of over two thousand leakage-controlled runs, and a recalibration analysis. That the same predictors carry substantially more signal within Korea (0.836 internally, 0.81-0.82 at matched sample size) than in the US (~0.77) is a concrete instance of the dataset-shift problem described for deployed clinical artificial intelligence [11,41], and underscores that high internally reported AUROCs do not guarantee cross-population usefulness.

### Plausible Mechanisms for the External Ceiling
Several lines of evidence converge on concept shift as the leading explanation for the ~0.77 external plateau. The first rules out modeling effort. Across 2,199 catalogued runs spanning feature engineering, hyperparameter search, ensembling, domain adaptation, alternative losses and deep tabular learners, no configuration selected on development-internal criteria exceeded that level, and apparent gains from externally tuned variants did not survive leakage control, leakage being the failure mode that has driven reproducibility problems across machine-learning-based science [42]; runs screened on external labels are identified as such in Multimedia Appendix 1. The second rules out estimation error: matching sample size did not close the gap, and increasing the training set from 0.5 to 2 million records slightly lowered external discrimination, a pattern consistent with source-specific overfitting rather than an estimation-limited ceiling.

The most direct test was an in-domain oracle, a deliberate departure from the external-validation rule, made for diagnosis only. Fitting NHANES on its own labels under cluster-separated cross-validation reached just 0.764-0.776, whereas the same predictors reached 0.81-0.82 in equally sized Korean samples. The transported model therefore already performs at the level reached by the best model that could be fitted on those predictors inside the external population, among the learners evaluated. That comparison is pooled across cycles while the transported estimates are cycle-specific, and the intervals overlap; it therefore locates the limit in the predictors rather than in the transfer only to the extent that the oracle is a fair upper bound.

Two specification analyses locate where the shortfall sits. At a PHQ-9 ≥5 threshold the Korea-US discrimination gap narrows from about 0.07 to about 0.02 (0.761 internally against 0.735 and 0.740 externally), and restricting training and evaluation to adults aged ≥30 years narrows it from 0.071 and 0.066 to 0.058 and 0.051. Both patterns place the shortfall at the moderate-or-worse end of the symptom distribution and among the youngest adults. Each analysis alters the target condition or the population as well as the gap; hence the pattern is suggestive rather than confirmatory.

Interpretability analysis points the same way. Predictor-importance rank order was almost unchanged between surveys (Spearman ρ=0.97 pre-pandemic and 0.99 post-pandemic), indicating that the transported model draws on the predictors in a similar order in both populations. Near-identical ordering alongside external discrimination about 0.07 lower is more consistent with predictors that are the right ones but weaker externally than with a model attending to the wrong ones. That is a statement about the fixed model's attribution and not about the external predictor-outcome associations, which the attribution never observes; it is a pattern that is at least consistent with the shift-stability framing of deployment failure in health artificial intelligence [43]. Two caveats bound this conclusion. Several predictors are measured differently in the two surveys, and the external cohorts are complete-case samples with differential depression-module non-response; hence measurement and selection effects cannot be separated from concept shift with these data.

### Clinical and Policy Implications
The intended use is triage, not diagnosis. At population prevalence the positive predictive value is low, and the appropriate reading of a positive score is a prompt for confirmatory assessment rather than an action in itself. Framed that way, the numbers are workable. At the single development-fixed operating point, positive predictive value was 0.147 in the pre-pandemic cohort and 0.214 in the post-pandemic cohort, ie, 6.8 and 4.7 respondents screened per case identified, and the model flagged 47% and 45% of the population for confirmatory assessment; net benefit exceeded both default strategies across the clinically relevant threshold range. The flag rate, not the positive predictive value, is the quantity against which a screening program must size its confirmatory capacity.

The six-item form is what makes web or mobile delivery realistic. All six items (self-rated health, age, sex, sleep duration, self-rated oral health, and partnership) are answerable on a single screen and require no examination, no laboratory value and no clinician; none is a symptom item, which keeps the triage step distinct from the full PHQ-9 it is meant to prioritize. The 17-predictor version additionally needs body mass index and two physician-diagnosed conditions, which a self-completed form can only take on trust.

Two deployment requirements follow from the calibration results. Any implementation outside the development population must apply local recalibration, since discrimination transported but absolute risk did not; intercept-plus-slope updating restored calibration in both external cohorts, each using that cohort's full sample, and is the recommended update; the local sample size needed for a stable update was not examined here; published minimum-sample-size derivations for external validation are driven by the calibration slope rather than the AUROC [44], and that constraint binds first at a small deployment site. Item reduction should also be re-evaluated whenever the deployment population differs materially from the development population, given that the ranking of predictors that supports a six-item form is itself population-dependent.

Equity requires explicit monitoring rather than a single fairness statement. Subgroup discrimination was lowest in Mexican American respondents in both NHANES cycles and, within the development population, in the highest income quintile, which is the one development subgroup that trips the analysis plan's 0.05 flag. The NHANES race and ethnicity intervals are wide and mutually overlapping, which leaves those differences unestablished; the highest-income KCHS estimate sits clear of the cohort interval. Either way, a deployed screener should report subgroup performance continuously rather than assume the development-time estimate holds, as recommended for machine learning intended to advance rather than erode health equity [45]. Three quantities warrant tracking: the subgroup AUROC, the subgroup calibration slope, and the false-negative rate at the operating threshold, which spans 0.03 to 0.31 across the subgroups reported here (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S7).

### Strengths and Limitations
The principal strength is that the outcome is identical in both surveys. Depression is defined as a nine-item PHQ-9 sum of at least 10 in KCHS and in NHANES alike, which removes the outcome-harmonization error that usually confounds cross-national validation and leaves country, health system, racial and ethnic composition, and survey mode as the differences under test. Validation covered two target periods as well as a second country, and recalibration was quantified rather than asserted. Evaluation was complex-survey weighted throughout, with intervals from a rescaled stratified cluster bootstrap validated against a closed-form design-based standard error. The predictor set was restricted to items both surveys measure in a form harmonizable to a common definition, with every inclusion decision documented and dated. The three candidate algorithms were compared at each one's tuned best under one identical protocol, after a preceding gradient-boosting-only search that is reported in full. A data-integrity audit and subsequent peer review identified and corrected seven data-processing and harmonization defects before the primary analysis, and the optimization search was leakage-controlled, which removes external-label selection as a source of optimism in the ceiling claim.

Several limitations bound the interpretation and are given in descending order of consequence. Depression-module non-response within the mobile examination center estimation frame cost 7.7% of the pre-pandemic and 14.1% of the post-pandemic sample, counting any unanswered item (14.6% and 33.3% on the wider interview frame; Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8), and was handled as complete-case; given that non-response was differential by education and by race and ethnicity, the external estimates, and particularly the post-pandemic subgroup results, may be biased in an unknown direction. Non-response is also the reason the PROBAST analysis domain is rated unclear for the external cohorts. Measurement is the second constraint: alcohol, physical activity, income, body mass index and food insecurity are measured differently in the two surveys and were harmonized to conservatively comparable definitions. Every predictor value used here was collected by interviewer administration or in an examination center; a web or mobile form changes the mode of administration for all 17 items, and the effect of that change on the reported performance is untested. The design is cross-sectional; hence the model screens current symptoms rather than predicting future onset. Living alone is unavailable in the pre-pandemic NHANES cycle, and food insecurity is a single item in KCHS and unmeasured there in 2020 and 2021. Prior depression diagnosis is not recorded in KCHS, which precluded a diagnosis-exclusion sensitivity analysis.

Four further constraints concern the analysis itself. Development used a 500,000-record training set and a 100,001-record hold-out drawn from the 2,048,510 pooled records rather than the full pooled sample; a training-set size experiment showed this did not cost external discrimination, but internal estimates are hold-out rather than fully cross-validated. The external cohorts carry 728 and 661 events, modest for a validation study and most limiting for calibration; the calibration slope is typically the binding precision constraint when the minimum sample size for external validation of a binary-outcome model is derived [44]; conditioning on a single fitted update rather than refitting it gives visibly wider slope intervals (0.82-1.19 pre and 0.88-1.13 post, Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S16); a deployment that fits its update once on a modest local sample is in that wider regime. The amendment that added three of the seventeen predictors was written while the study was under way, after an earlier external result was known but before any model using the amended set was fitted; it is dated, summarized in English in Multimedia Appendix 1 with the unmodified Korean original released alongside the code, and its contribution is reported as a robustness analysis rather than a primary result. Finally, two of the four hypotheses, H2 and H3, were stated qualitatively in the protocol, without numeric decision margins; thus judging those two "supported" involves interpretation.

A competing contributor is partly quantified, and it is not concept shift. The transportable predictor set is bounded by the development survey's content. In a coverage analysis, candidate domains available in NHANES but not harmonizable to KCHS were extracted: physician-diagnosed multimorbidity (asthma, arthritis, thyroid, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, liver disease, and cancer, excluding circulatory conditions) and healthcare utilization; adding those domains raised the NHANES in-domain oracle from 0.765 to 0.778 (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S14). KCHS, however, records only hypertension and diabetes among physician-diagnosed conditions across more than one survey year, both already included (dyslipidemia, osteoporosis, cataract and arthritis are fielded in a single year and therefore cannot enter a model pooled over 2017-2025), and does not collect a broader chronic-disease inventory, hospitalization, or visit counts; these domains therefore cannot enter a KCHS-developed transportable model. Part of the residual external gap is thus attributable to development-survey coverage rather than to concept shift alone, and a development survey that captured multimorbidity and utilization might raise the attainable ceiling, though the 0.013 increment observed here is an in-domain NHANES gain and is not itself evidence that the added signal would transport from Korea.

### Conclusions
A survey-derived depression screener transports across country and pandemic with clinically useful discrimination and recoverable calibration. The cross-national ceiling near 0.77 held against every leakage-controlled attempt to exceed it and against an in-domain oracle fitted on the external labels. It is therefore best read as a property of the transportable predictor set rather than as a proven maximum, most consistent with shift in the predictor-outcome relationship and with residual contributions from differing measurement, development-survey coverage and complete-case selection. One practical consequence follows: for a screener of this kind, the remaining gains are more likely to come from predictor domains a development survey does not yet collect than from further algorithm search. A low-burden six-item version, paired with local recalibration and continuing subgroup monitoring, is a plausible scalable digital triage aid.
