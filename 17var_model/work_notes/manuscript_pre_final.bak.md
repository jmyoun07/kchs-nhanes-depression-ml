## Abstract

**Background:** Depression is a leading cause of disability, and scalable, low-cost screening is needed. Most machine learning (ML) depression models from national surveys report internal validation only; whether such models transport to another country and remain stable across the COVID-19 shift is rarely tested.

**Objective:** To develop an ML model for current depression (Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9] ≥10) in the Korea Community Health Survey (KCHS), externally validate it in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) across the pre- and post-COVID-19 periods as the engine for a digital triage tool, and determine whether any external shortfall is closable by modeling.

**Methods:** Elastic net, random forest and gradient boosting were developed in KCHS (2017-2025; pooled n=2,048,510; cluster-level split into a 500,000-record training set and a 100,001-record hold-out sharing no cluster) using a harmonized 17-predictor set shared with NHANES, one item of which is unavailable in the pre-pandemic NHANES file. Primary-model fitting, tuning and selection used KCHS-internal cross-validation only; the trained model was applied to NHANES without refitting. Evaluation was complex-survey-weighted with rescaled cluster-bootstrap CIs, plus Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) and subgroup fairness. Performance was contrasted between the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS)-defined pre-pandemic (n=7,888) and post-pandemic (n=5,210) cycles. A leakage-controlled run registry and an in-domain oracle fitted on NHANES labels characterized attainable external discrimination.

**Results:** Gradient boosting (LightGBM) was primary. Internal area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in clusters never used for training was 0.836 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.827-0.846), meeting the ≥0.80 target (H1). External discrimination was lower yet clinically useful, and the two pandemic periods were indistinguishable (pre 0.765, 95% CI 0.724-0.804; post 0.771, 0.739-0.801; difference +0.006, 95% CI -0.045 to +0.056, computed post hoc); calibration deteriorated (slope 0.65-0.68; calibration-in-the-large [CITL] +0.51 and +1.13, under-prediction) and was restored by intercept/slope recalibration to slope 1.00 (95% CI 0.95-1.00) and 1.00 (0.97-1.00) with CITL 0.00 (H2, H3 supported). The analysis plan's parsimony margin, applied entirely inside the development data, selected a 6-item model (0.822 internal, 0.748 pre, 0.757 post; H4 supported). No leakage-controlled configuration among 2,199 catalogued runs exceeded ~0.77 externally, and the in-domain oracle reached only 0.764-0.776, whereas the same predictors reached 0.81-0.82 in equally sized Korean samples.

**Conclusions:** A Korea-developed depression screener transported to the US and across both pandemic periods with clinically useful discrimination and calibration recoverable by intercept/slope updating. The ~0.77 cross-national ceiling survived sample size, algorithm choice and covariate-shift correction, and is most consistent with a property of the transportable predictor set rather than a modeling failure. A low-burden six-item version with local recalibration is a plausible scalable digital triage aid.

**Keywords:** depression; mass screening; machine learning; external validation; transportability; COVID-19; mobile health; Republic of Korea.

## Introduction

Depression is among the leading causes of years lived with disability worldwide [1], and its detection at the community and primary-care level remains incomplete. Brief self-report instruments such as the PHQ-9 enable population-scale screening [2,3], yet a fixed cut-off applied uniformly across heterogeneous populations does not prioritize those at highest risk, even where universal screening of adults is recommended [4]. Risk stratification from routinely available sociodemographic and behavioral information could support more efficient triage, and a tool restricted to self-reportable items can be delivered as a web or mobile form at low marginal cost. National health-examination and community-health surveys already collect these items and the relevant clinical ones, and community-health surveillance in particular reaches hundreds of thousands of respondents a year, an attractive development base.

ML models for depression have been developed repeatedly from large national surveys, including the KCHS and the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) [5,6], typically reporting strong internal discrimination. Most published prediction models are assessed through internal validation alone, in depression modeling and in other clinical domains alike [7,8]. Internal performance is an optimistic indicator of real-world behavior: it does not establish that a model transports to a different sampling frame, health system, or population [9], and it does not test stability under temporal distribution shift.

The COVID-19 pandemic constitutes a natural distribution shift for mental-health prediction [10,11]: the distributions of depressive symptoms and of the associated behavioral characteristics changed, and Korean national surveillance recorded a rise in depressive symptoms and suicide attempts over the pandemic years [12]. A screening model carried into a shifted target population may show degraded calibration even where discrimination is preserved. Whether recalibration, ie, updating the intercept and slope alone [13,14], restores clinical usefulness is a practically important, under-studied question for deployed screening tools.

This study addresses these gaps by validating a KCHS-developed model in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Depression is operationalized identically in both surveys, minimizing outcome-harmonization error, while country, health system, racial/ethnic composition, and survey mode differ, which makes NHANES a substantively external validation set. NHANES additionally provides NCHS-defined pre- and post-pandemic data, allowing the transported model to be evaluated in two periods of the same external population; since KCHS development pools 2017-2025 and spans both, this contrasts target periods rather than validating a model frozen before the later cycle.

Two questions follow, neither settled by the models and reviews cited above [5-8]. The first is empirical: how much discrimination is lost when a model developed in one national survey is applied without refitting to another country's survey, and does that loss change across a population-wide shock such as the pandemic? The second is interpretive: when external performance falls, is the shortfall a modeling failure that better algorithms or larger training sets could recover, or a property of the transportable predictor set that no modeling effort can close? Separating the two requires more than a single external estimate. It requires an estimate of what the same predictors can achieve when a model is fitted directly inside the external population, together with a modeling search in which no external label is used for selection.

This study pursued five aims: (1) develop an ML depression-screening model in KCHS; (2) externally validate it in US NHANES (geographic transportability); (3) evaluate it separately in the pre- and post-COVID-19 cycles and quantify recalibration (transportability across target periods); (4) derive a parsimonious model for a low-burden digital tool; and (5) determine whether any residual external shortfall is closable by modeling, using a catalogued leakage-controlled optimization registry and an in-domain oracle fitted inside the external population. The pre-specified hypotheses attach to aims 1 to 4: H1 the full model achieves internal AUROC ≥0.80; H2 external discrimination remains clinically useful while calibration may deteriorate; H3 recalibration substantially improves calibration; H4 the parsimonious model incurs only a small performance loss. Aim 5 carried no numeric hypothesis and is reported as a diagnostic analysis.

## Methods

### Study Design and Data Sources

This retrospective secondary-data study developed a clinical prediction model in one national survey and validated it, without refitting, in another. Reporting follows Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis extended to artificial intelligence (TRIPOD+AI, 2024) [15], and risk of bias was self-assessed with the Prediction model Risk Of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) [16] as overall low-to-unclear, driven by complete-case handling of substantial and differential depression-module non-response in the external cohorts (Multimedia Appendix 1, Part B). The protocol and statistical analysis plan, including the four hypotheses, were written and dated before any analysis; the study was not registered in a public registry.

Development used the Korea Community Health Survey, a nationwide Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) survey [17] of community-dwelling adults, with annual in-person household interviews (~230,000/year) under a stratified, clustered, weighted complex-sample design; the pooled development sample was KCHS 2017-2025 records with the PHQ-9 administered (n=2,048,510). The internal split was made at cluster level (survey year crossed with design stratum crossed with primary sampling unit): the 100,001-record hold-out and the 500,000-record training set share 0 clusters. The training-set size was fixed for computational tractability before any external evaluation (split procedure and size experiment in Multimedia Appendix 1, Part B).

External validation used NHANES, an NCHS survey combining household interview and mobile examination center assessment; depression was measured with the Depression Screener (DPQ; PHQ-9) module. NCHS documents the 2015-2018 sample design and estimation procedures [18]; the cycle-specific documentation and weight variables are named in Multimedia Appendix 1. Two periods were used as separate validation sets: pre-pandemic (2017-March 2020, "P_" files; n=7,888) and post-pandemic (August 2021-August 2023, "_L" files; n=5,210).

### Participants and Outcome

Eligible respondents in both surveys were adults aged 20 years or older with all nine PHQ-9 items answered; pregnant respondents were retained in the main analysis. Pooling KCHS 2017-2025 gave 2,048,510 eligible records. Of examined adults aged 20 years or older, 7,888 pre-pandemic and 5,210 post-pandemic answered all nine items. Counts at each stage of the NHANES frame and item-level non-response are tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8. Predictors and outcome were recorded in the same survey contact, hence measured contemporaneously; blinding does not apply to secondary analysis of already-collected survey responses.

The outcome is current depression, defined as a PHQ-9 total of at least 10 and computed identically in both surveys (nine items, scored 0 to 3, KCHS items recoded 1-4→0-3 to match NHANES). The ≥10 cut-off is the conventional screening threshold, evaluated against semi-structured diagnostic interviews in individual-participant-data meta-analyses [3,19] and validated in a Korean population [20].

### Predictors and Harmonization

Only variables reducible to a common definition in both surveys entered the harmonized model, which comprised 17 predictors (Table 1). All 616 KCHS concepts were crosswalked against the NHANES codebook for both cycles; concepts were retained that are (i) available in NHANES, (ii) collected in more than one KCHS survey year, and (iii) not circular with the PHQ-9 outcome. Those criteria use survey documentation and variable availability only, and no NHANES outcome association was estimated for any candidate before the amendment described below was locked. The retained domains have established associations with depression in the epidemiological literature: socioeconomic position [21], sleep duration [22], physical activity [23], body mass index (BMI) [24], smoking [25], living alone [26] and partnership status [27].

Employment, self-rated oral health and household food insecurity entered the set through a protocol amendment written and dated before any model using those items was fitted; the study protocol had specified the other fourteen. Each addition has an established association with depression: food insecurity (pooled odds ratio ≈1.4) [28], unemployment (≈1.9-2.1) [29] and poor oral health (a bidirectional relationship) [30]. The crosswalk available when the amendment was written held a 175-variable extract, of which the seventeen predictors are every eligible concept; a later exhaustive extraction identified 265 variables common to both cycles. Five further harmonizable items from that extraction were screened post hoc against NHANES outcome associations; none was added (Multimedia Appendix 1, Part A).

Living alone is the single retained item not present in both cycles and has no missing values anywhere in the KCHS training data; the tree learner therefore never learns a missing direction for it, and the pre-pandemic cohort is scored as if no respondent lived alone. The pandemic-period contrast is repeated with living alone removed (Sensitivity Analyses).

Three harmonization cautions attach to the set. BMI is self-reported in KCHS, measured in NHANES. Alcohol, physical activity, and income use different instruments and were reduced to conservatively comparable definitions, only alcohol being variable in sensitivity analysis. A direct Korea-US comparison using the same 18-item household module found food insecurity associated with higher odds of PHQ-9 depression in both countries, with a larger association in Korea [31]; the single-item KCHS measure used here is therefore the weakest link in the harmonized set rather than a difference in the underlying construct. Scale differences between the surveys and questionnaire discontinuities within KCHS were corrected in the analytic data before the primary analysis rather than carried as limitations; the corrections are itemized in the data-integrity audit reported below, described in Multimedia Appendix 1 and quantified singly in Table S10.

All missing-data handling was performed by a preprocessing pipeline fitted in the development training set and applied unchanged to the internal hold-out and to each external set (no leakage). The income quintile is the one transformation not carried over unchanged, being a rank-based score recomputed within each dataset and, inside KCHS, within each survey year. Remaining preprocessing detail, including two alternative imputation strategies examined in sensitivity analysis, is in Multimedia Appendix 1.

### Model Development and Evaluation

No a priori sample-size calculation was performed; the samples are those the data allow, each external cohort being every eligible respondent in that cycle. What that supplies is reported instead: approximately 18,000 events in the training set, about 1,000 per candidate predictor and far above conventional events-per-predictor requirements, and approximately 3,600 in the hold-out. The external cohorts (728 events pre, 661 post; weighted prevalence 8.4% and 12.2%) are the binding constraint on precision; discrimination therefore carries confidence intervals, and subgroup estimates are read as exploratory.

Three algorithms were considered, penalized logistic regression (elastic net), random forest and gradient boosting (LightGBM), in two selection steps confined to the KCHS training set. First, a randomized search over 24 LightGBM configurations crossed with two feature representations (main effects with and without four candidate interaction terms) fixed the gradient-boosting hyperparameters; the main-effects representation won, and the interactions did not improve cross-validated discrimination. Second, all three were tuned over comparable grids under one identical cross-validation protocol and compared at each algorithm's best configuration, following evidence that machine learning shows no systematic advantage over regression once validation bias is controlled [32]; only gradient boosting had received the preceding search, hence the comparison is like-for-like at tuned best rather than equal in cumulative effort.

The analysis plan fixed the primary-model rule in advance: highest cross-validated AUROC, with differences smaller than 0.005 treated as ties and broken by proximity of the calibration slope to 1, and only then by parsimony. The pipeline as executed ranked the algorithms on AUROC and then calibration slope without constructing the tie band; the plan's rule, checked retrospectively, reproduces the selected model (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S9). Two departures are recorded in the same table: no class weighting, resampling or other imbalance correction was applied, the learner being fitted on the natural 3.6% event rate; and the searches were run single-pass rather than as 5-fold cross-validation repeated five times, which matters here, since the two leading algorithms differ by less than the tie band.

No NHANES data were used in fitting, tuning or selecting the primary model. The in-domain oracle reported under Testing Whether External Discrimination Can Be Raised Above ~0.77 is fitted on NHANES labels by design, as a diagnostic upper bound rather than a candidate model. The predictor set is the one exception: three of the seventeen items entered through a dated amendment written after the external result for the earlier set was known (Predictors and Harmonization). Complex-survey weights were handled by unweighted training with weighted evaluation (probed in sensitivity analysis).

Performance was summarized by discrimination (AUROC and area under the precision-recall curve [AUPRC]), calibration, and operating characteristics, all complex-survey weighted. Calibration was assessed at the moderate level of the calibration hierarchy [33] and summarized by the calibration slope, calibration-in-the-large (CITL), the Brier score and expected calibration error (ECE), CITL being the intercept of a weighted offset logistic model. Ninety-five percent CIs are reported for AUROC, AUPRC and, externally, the calibration slope and CITL as transported and after recalibration; ECE, operating characteristics and net benefit are point estimates (ECE for the reason given with Table S16). CIs were obtained by a rescaled stratified cluster bootstrap with 1000 replicates [34], for cohort-level and subgroup estimates alike; bootstrap P values are two-sided, the smallest attainable value at 1000 replicates being reported as P<.001. Full development and resampling detail is given in Multimedia Appendix 1 and in the released analysis code and model card (see Data Availability).

A single operating threshold was fixed once in the development data at the predicted probability giving 85% sensitivity (0.02051) and applied unchanged to the internal hold-out and to both external cohorts; the external operating characteristics are therefore transported values rather than values selected in the cohort where reported. Clinical utility was assessed by weighted decision-curve analysis [35] over threshold probabilities of 0.02 to 0.30, each dataset carrying its own screen-all and screen-none default strategies.

### External Validation and Sensitivity Analyses

Geographic transportability was assessed by applying the KCHS-trained model to NHANES without refitting, and transportability across target periods by evaluating the same transported model separately in the pre- and post-pandemic cycles; since KCHS development pools 2017-2025 and spans both periods, the second comparison contrasts target periods rather than validating a model frozen before the later cycle. Recovery of calibration was quantified by out-of-sample intercept and slope updating, which corrects calibration without re-estimating any predictor effect [13,14] and is the conventional first step for a model moved to a new population [36]. Following the plan, three updates were applied, each cross-fitted over five folds grouped by primary sampling unit: intercept only, intercept plus slope, and one-parameter temperature scaling with the intercept fixed at zero. Table 2 tabulates the intercept-plus-slope result, the recommended update; the other two steps appear numerically in the Results.

Three further analyses were undertaken: interpretability, through global importance from tree-based Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) [37] and cross-survey rank concordance of predictor importance (Spearman correlation between KCHS and each NHANES cycle); fairness, through weighted subgroup AUROC and calibration slope across sex, age, education, and (NHANES) race/ethnicity, since algorithmic performance can differ systematically across groups [38]; and a parsimonious model for a low-burden self-administered form. For that model, predictor order and item count were both fixed inside the development training set, split into a 450,000-record fitting set and a 50,000-record selection set: predictors were entered in descending order of global SHAP importance, the model was refitted at every item count from 1 to 17, and the count was chosen on selection-set AUROC by the analysis plan's margin (the smallest set within 0.02 AUROC of the full model); a variant using a stricter 0.01 margin is flagged as post hoc (Multimedia Appendix 1, Part D). The internal hold-out and both NHANES cohorts served for evaluation only.

The model is intended as a triage aid for population-level screening programs and web or mobile self-assessment services, operated by public-health and primary-care services rather than by individuals acting alone, to prioritize who receives a full PHQ-9 and clinical follow-up. It is not a diagnostic instrument.

Sensitivity analyses were pre-specified, with further analyses added afterwards: the outcome cut-off (≥5 as well as ≥10), removal of the three added predictors (→14) and of food insecurity alone (→16), weighted versus unweighted training, two alternative imputation schemes, an age lower bound of 30 years applied to training *and* evaluation, a protocol-specified continuous-score analysis of how the model ranks the untransformed PHQ-9 total, and three alternative alcohol definitions. Weighted AUROC is tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S5 for every specification apart from the continuous-score and alcohol analyses, reported in the Results text.

All analyses were performed in Python 3.14, with package versions listed in the model card. Net benefit is reported without intervals, since inference on a decision-analytic quantity answers a different question from the decision itself [39].

An adversarial data-integrity audit corrected three silent data-processing defects before final analysis: a fabricated living-alone constant in NHANES pre-pandemic, a post-pandemic physical-activity unit-field parsing error, and an income refusal code (77777) not treated as missing. Peer review identified four further defects, in the post-pandemic income quintile, the 2017 income refusal codes, the post-2020 drinking classification and the 2018 physical-activity indicator. The four peer-review defects were corrected in the primary analysis rather than carried forward as limitations; the defects and remedies are itemized in Multimedia Appendix 1, and Table S10 quantifies six corrections one at a time.

## Results

### Participant Characteristics
The pooled KCHS development sample comprised 2,048,510 adults and the external cohorts 7,888 pre-pandemic and 5,210 post-pandemic NHANES adults, similar in mean age (49.4, 48.4 and 48.8 years) and in sex composition (50.5%, 51.5% and 50.8% female) but not in outcome frequency. Weighted depression prevalence (PHQ-9 ≥10) was 3.6% in KCHS against 8.4% and 12.2% in the two NHANES cycles (Figure 1). The surveys also differed markedly on measured covariates: mean BMI 23.7 (KCHS) vs 29.8 and 29.7 (NHANES pre and post), and weighted food-insecurity prevalence 3.7% in KCHS vs 16.4% and 17.2% in the NHANES cycles, computed among respondents with the item recorded (missing 22.1%, 6.0% and 6.3%, respectively; every characteristic in Table S4 uses available-case denominators). This pattern is consistent with genuinely distinct populations and, for food insecurity, with the single-item-vs-module measurement difference.

Depression-module non-response was appreciable in both NHANES cycles and larger after the pandemic. The relevant denominator is the mobile examination center (MEC) frame, since the depression module is administered there and every weighted estimate in this study uses MEC weights: of 8,544 pre-pandemic and 6,064 post-pandemic MEC-examined adults ≥20 years, 7.7% and 14.1%, respectively, were lost to PHQ-9 item non-response, yielding the complete-case analytic samples of 7,888 and 5,210 (item-level detail in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8). The corresponding three-or-more-item rates are 7.4% and 13.3% on the MEC frame, against 14.3% and 32.7% when counted over all interviewed adults; that second denominator conflates module non-response with never having been examined, which the MEC weights already account for. Non-response was differential: higher among respondents with less than high-school education (12.9% pre, 26.6% post, MEC frame) and among Asian and Black respondents; hence the external cohorts, and particularly the post-pandemic fairness estimates, should be read as complete-case results. Reassuringly, adding respondents missing only one or two items, with the missing items filled by the person-mean, changed the observed prevalence by ≤0.1 percentage points in both cycles (unweighted: 9.23%→9.27% pre, 12.69%→12.78% post). Exact values are tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8.

### Model Development and Internal Performance
Under the identical tuning protocol, cross-validated AUROC in the training set was 0.840 for LightGBM, 0.835 for random forest and 0.822 for elastic net; the two leading algorithms fell inside the analysis plan's 0.005 tie band, which the calibration-slope tie-break resolves in favor of LightGBM (cross-validated slope 1.00 against 1.20), while elastic net lay outside it (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S9). LightGBM was therefore the primary model, and the plan's tie band, reconstructed after the fact, reproduces the choice the pipeline made. Internal hold-out AUROC was 0.836 (95% CI 0.827-0.846), AUPRC 0.246, Brier 0.030, calibration slope 1.03, CITL +0.10; H1 met. Given that the split is at cluster level, this is an estimate for clusters the model never saw.

### External Validation Across Country and Pandemic Period
External discrimination fell by about 0.07 AUROC from the internal estimate and differed little between the two cycles: pre 0.765 (95% CI 0.724-0.804), post 0.771 (0.739-0.801) (Table 2; Figures 2 and 3); the difference is +0.006 (95% CI -0.045 to +0.056; bootstrap P=.86), an interval computed after the fact, since no between-cycle test was pre-specified; the data are therefore consistent with no deterioration across the pandemic but cannot exclude a modest change. AUPRC moved in the opposite direction (0.246 internally, 0.262 pre and 0.345 post), which follows from the higher external event rate rather than from better external ranking: precision-recall summaries rise with prevalence, and each value should be read against its own no-skill baseline of 3.6%, 8.4% and 12.2%. Calibration degraded in both cycles: the slope fell to 0.65 (pre) and 0.68 (post) and CITL rose to +0.51 and +1.13, indicating that the Korea-trained model systematically *under*-predicted absolute risk in the US, as expected from the prevalence difference (KCHS 3.6% vs 8.4% and 12.2%). Of the three pre-specified recalibration steps applied out-of-sample, intercept-only updating removed the mean bias but left the slope unchanged (0.65 pre, 0.68 post), intercept-plus-slope updating restored both (slope 1.00 and 1.00, CITL 0.00), and temperature scaling was intermediate, leaving the slope at 0.83 pre and 1.17 post with CITL +0.02 and -0.02; intercept-plus-slope is therefore the recommended deployment update; H2 and H3 supported.

### Testing Whether External Discrimination Can Be Raised Above ~0.77
Leakage-controlled optimization (2,199 recorded runs across 117 experiment files, catalogued in the released registry: feature engineering, hyperparameter search, heterogeneous ensembling, transfer-robust internal selection, domain adaptation, alternative losses, and structural variants) did not exceed external AUROC ~0.77 when model selection was restricted to KCHS-internal criteria (Multimedia Appendix 1, Tables S11 and S12). Apparent gains from externally tuned variants (eg, specific interaction terms, ensemble weights) did not survive leakage control.

The ceiling was localized with an in-domain oracle: fitting NHANES on its own labels, a deliberate departure from the external-validation rule, made for diagnosis only. Across logistic, penalized-logistic, histogram-gradient-boosting and LightGBM learners, the oracle was cross-validated with folds split by primary sampling unit within each cycle so that no cluster appeared in both training and validation, and reached only 0.764-0.776 (best learner 0.776, 95% CI 0.753-0.799; widening to 0.745-0.794 when the oracle is refitted inside each bootstrap replicate so that model-fitting variability is included; Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S15). The KCHS-transferred model (pre 0.765, post 0.771) therefore sits inside the confidence interval of the best in-domain performance any of these learners achieved when fitted directly to NHANES. This is an empirical benchmark over the learners evaluated, not a proof of a maximum; it is estimated on the pooled pre+post sample whereas the transported figures are cycle-specific, and its interval overlaps the transported estimates; thus the comparison is approximate and cannot exclude a modest true gap. Matching sample size does not close the gap: reducing the Korean data to the NHANES sample size and cross-validating within it still yielded 0.823 at n=7,888 (the pre-pandemic cohort size) and 0.810 at n=13,098 (the pooled oracle size), rising to 0.830 only at n=200,000 (best of three learners, 5-fold weighted AUROC, in a separate KCHS-internal sample-size experiment). At identical n, in other words, the same 17 predictors carry 0.81-0.82 of discrimination in Korea against an NHANES ceiling of ~0.77.

Three further checks converged on the same conclusion: (i) covariate-shift correction by density-ratio reweighting of the KCHS training sample toward the NHANES covariate distribution (labels unused) *reduced* external AUROC, to 0.766 and 0.758 against the baseline of the exploration run that anchors Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S11 (0.772 pre, 0.769 post), showing that this particular correction did not help, which weakens but does not exclude a covariate-shift explanation; (ii) increasing the KCHS training set from 0.5 million to 2 million records did not raise, and slightly lowered, external discrimination, consistent with source-specific overfitting rather than an estimation-limited ceiling; and (iii) adding the remaining harmonized-but-unused survey items (public-assistance receipt, weekend sleep duration, alcohol frequency, alcohol quantity in two codings and binge drinking) changed external AUROC by at most 0.003 in either direction, a check evaluated on external data and therefore reported as a diagnostic rather than as leakage-controlled evidence. These are changes against the in-run baseline of that same exploration run and are not comparable in level with the primary estimates. Deep tabular learners (two multilayer perceptrons, TabNet, and an FT-Transformer) were additionally trained and transferred; all fell below gradient boosting internally and none exceeded it on both external cohorts (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S13), consistent with the model-agnostic ceiling.

Sample size, model choice, covariate imbalance and omitted harmonized information were each tested and none closed the gap, and the gap is not closable by recalibration either, which corrects calibration rather than rank ordering. The most consistent remaining explanation is shift in the predictor-outcome relationship itself (concept shift), which these data cannot separate from the surveys' differing predictor measurement and from complete-case selection; the interpretation is developed in the Discussion.

### Parsimonious Deployment Model
Predictor order and item count were both fixed inside the training set, and the analysis plan's margin (the smallest set within 0.02 AUROC of the full model) selected six items: self-rated health, age, sex, sleep duration, self-rated oral health, partnership. Evaluated afterwards on data that played no part in selection, this six-item form achieved hold-out AUROC 0.822, NHANES pre 0.748 and NHANES post 0.757, against 0.837/0.766/0.769 for the full 17-predictor model fitted on the same data (a loss of 0.015 internally, 0.018 pre-pandemic and 0.012 post-pandemic, each within the plan's 0.02 tolerance); H4 supported. Utility followed the same pattern: at a within-cohort operating point fixed to 85% sensitivity (apparent values, Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S2), the six-item form required 7.9 (pre) and 5.7 (post) respondents screened per case detected against 7.7 and 5.3 for the 17-predictor model, and its net benefit exceeded both default strategies in every cohort at threshold probabilities of 0.10 and 0.15 (Table S3). On the selection set, discrimination rose steeply and then flattened (1 item 0.741; 3 items 0.781; 5 items 0.813; 6 items 0.818; 8 items 0.825; 12 items 0.832; 17 items 0.833), the margin being applied over every count below 17 without imposing the plan's preferred 5-10 item range. The five-item set falls 0.0002 below the threshold and the six-item set clears it by 0.0044; the five-versus-six boundary is therefore decided at the fourth decimal and should not be read as robust, and the five-item model reaches 0.737 pre-pandemic and 0.751 post-pandemic externally. The stricter-margin seven-item variant is reported only as an exploratory comparison; no external information entered the item-count decision itself. The full k=1-17 trajectory is tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S6.

### Sensitivity Analyses
Discrimination was stable across specifications that alter modeling choices rather than the target condition (Figure 4): weighted training (0.836/0.764/0.768 internal/pre/post), missing-indicator imputation (0.837/0.764/0.772), single chained-equations imputation of the continuous predictors (0.837/0.763/0.770), and predictor-set variants (below) all lay close to the primary model internally. Restricting both training and evaluation to respondents aged ≥30 years *improved* discrimination (0.838/0.781/0.787), consistent with the lower subgroup performance observed at ages 20-39; in an additional variant restricting training alone, discrimination was 0.836/0.770/0.769, which places most of that improvement in the evaluation case mix rather than in what the model learned. Changing the outcome definition to PHQ-9 ≥5 lowered discrimination throughout (0.761/0.735/0.740) at a much higher weighted prevalence (15.6%/25.0%/32.1%), as expected when the target shifts from moderate-or-worse symptoms to mild or greater symptom burden; notably, the Korea-US gap narrows from about 0.07 AUROC at the ≥10 threshold to about 0.02 at ≥5 (0.761 vs 0.735-0.740), which is consistent with, but does not establish, a larger cross-national difference at the more severe end of the symptom distribution. Replacing current drinking with drinking at least twice weekly, with binge drinking, or with all three indicators together (the alternative alcohol definitions listed in the analysis plan) changed discrimination by at most 0.003 externally, the alternatives reaching 0.769-0.771 pre-pandemic and 0.770 post-pandemic against an in-run baseline of 0.772 and 0.769; that run predates the data-integrity corrections and the cluster-level split, hence its levels are not comparable with the primary estimates or with Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S5. Each harmonization correction was additionally assessed singly against the uncorrected baseline, in which the other corrections are not yet applied (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S10); no single tabulated correction, the age top-code alignment and the rank-based income-quintile rebuild included, changed weighted AUROC by more than 0.003 in any cohort.

The harmonized set is also robust to its least-established members: refitting without employment, self-rated oral health and food insecurity (the three items whose inclusion is documented in the protocol amendment) lowers external discrimination to 0.745 (pre) and 0.757 (post) when each model is given its own hyperparameter search, a difference of Δ+0.020, 95% CI +0.010 to +0.030 and Δ+0.014, 95% CI +0.006 to +0.020 (P<.001 for both; paired rescaled cluster bootstrap). Under the common configuration of Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S5, where the 17-, 16- and 14-predictor models share one setting and the 14-predictor value is 0.758 post-pandemic, removing food insecurity alone accounts for 28% of the 17-versus-14 gap pre-pandemic and 50% post-pandemic. In the pre-specified continuous-score analysis, run in the same pre-correction generation as the alcohol analysis and on a record-level rather than cluster-level split, a classifier of the deployed specification ranked the untransformed PHQ-9 total with weighted Spearman ρ=0.378 internally, 0.403 pre-pandemic and 0.439 post-pandemic, and a model trained directly on the continuous score performed similarly (ρ=0.402/0.403/0.424; weighted R² 0.216/0.166/0.102); thus the transportability pattern is not an artifact of dichotomizing the outcome. Data-integrity fixes did not alter substantive conclusions. Exact values for the specification analyses are tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S5, and the effect of each harmonization correction applied singly in Table S10.

Living alone is recorded in every KCHS year and in the post-pandemic NHANES cycle but is absent from the pre-pandemic file, and it has no missing values in the training data, which leaves the learner routing the missing pre-pandemic column to the same branch as "not living alone" (Predictors and Harmonization). The pandemic-period contrast therefore mixes a period difference with a predictor-availability difference. Refitting the model on the 16 predictors observed in both cycles removes that difference and leaves the contrast unchanged: external AUROC 0.765 pre-pandemic and 0.771 post-pandemic, a difference of +0.006 (95% CI -0.044 to +0.056) against +0.006 (-0.045 to +0.056) for the 17-predictor model, with internal discrimination 0.836 against 0.836. The availability difference therefore does not account for the cross-cycle result.

Response-propensity weighting gave the same answer: reweighting each external cohort by the inverse of its education-specific depression-module response rate moved external AUROC by at most 0.002 (0.765 vs 0.765 pre-pandemic and 0.769 vs 0.771 post-pandemic); differential non-response by education therefore does not by itself explain the external level.

### Interpretability, Fairness, and Clinical Utility
Global SHAP importance, computed in the development fitting partition, ranked the predictors: self-rated health (0.59); age (0.37); sex (0.28); sleep duration (0.26); self-rated oral health (0.23); partnership (0.19); smoking (0.17). Food insecurity contributed modestly (0.06, ranked 10th). The predictors carrying most of the signal are therefore the general-health, demographic and sleep items that both surveys measure identically (Figure 5). The rank ordering of predictor importance was highly concordant between KCHS and both NHANES cycles (Spearman ρ=0.97 pre, 0.99 post), indicating that the transported model draws on the predictors in a similar order in both populations. This is a statement about how the fixed model attributes risk, not about the external predictor-outcome associations, since the attribution uses no external outcome; it therefore constrains, without establishing, the interpretation that the limit lies in the strength rather than the structure of those associations. The clearest exception was food insecurity, which ranked 10th in KCHS but 8th in NHANES pre and on which the transported model leaned substantially harder externally (mean absolute SHAP 0.06 in KCHS against 0.18 and 0.15 in the NHANES cycles), consistent with its different measurement (single item vs 10-item module) and much higher prevalence in the US.

Subgroup discrimination in KCHS spanned 0.781-0.882 (weighted AUROC): sex (female 0.826, 95% CI 0.814-0.837; male 0.838, 0.822-0.854), education (0.811-0.882), age (20-39 years 0.808, 0.790-0.827 < 40-59 years 0.822, 0.801-0.842 < ≥60 years 0.860, 0.850-0.870), and household income quintile (0.781-0.835, lowest in the highest-income quintile). In NHANES, discrimination was lower and far less precisely estimated. Pre-pandemic: Mexican American 0.693 (0.633-0.763; n=914), non-Hispanic Black 0.762 (0.727-0.805; n=2,096), other/multiracial 0.764 (0.665-0.865; n=381), non-Hispanic White 0.769 (0.715-0.818; n=2,798), other Hispanic 0.773 (0.725-0.820; n=813), non-Hispanic Asian 0.807 (0.716-0.874; n=886). Post-pandemic: Mexican American 0.692 (0.533-0.802; n=330), non-Hispanic Black 0.726 (0.642-0.791; n=598), non-Hispanic White 0.773 (0.726-0.815; n=3,215), other/multiracial 0.782 (0.701-0.853; n=340), other Hispanic 0.789 (0.751-0.845; n=499), non-Hispanic Asian 0.845 (0.656-0.987; n=228). Applying the fairness flag set in the analysis plan (a subgroup AUROC differing from the cohort value by more than 0.05) flags the highest income quintile in KCHS (-0.056); Mexican American in NHANES pre-pandemic (-0.072); Mexican American in NHANES post-pandemic (-0.078); non-Hispanic Asian in NHANES post-pandemic (+0.074). Within KCHS a few intervals sit clear of the cohort interval: higher in adults aged ≥60 years (0.850-0.870), middle-school-educated respondents (0.861-0.902); lower in income quintile 4 (0.772-0.822), the highest income quintile (0.744-0.818). Subgroup discrimination is therefore not uniform in the development population. In NHANES, by contrast, the intervals are wide and mutually overlapping, and every one overlaps the cohort-level interval; hence the race/ethnicity ordering should not be read as established differential performance; that the lowest point estimate falls in Mexican American respondents in both cycles nonetheless warrants monitoring as an equity caveat (Figure 6). Internal subgroup calibration was good (slope 0.96-1.13 across sex, age, education and income strata; subgroup CITL -0.03 to +0.20). In NHANES the subgroup calibration slopes were below 1 in all but one subgroup (0.57-0.79; non-Hispanic Asian post-pandemic 1.19), and subgroup CITL was positive in every group except Mexican American pre-pandemic (up to +1.32), confirming that the external miscalibration is under-prediction of absolute risk and not only over-extreme ranking. Exact subgroup values with CIs are tabulated in Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S7. 

On decision-curve analysis (weighted, with cohort-specific screen-all and screen-none default strategies), the model provided positive net benefit exceeding both defaults across a clinically relevant threshold-probability range in all three datasets (Figure 7). At a threshold probability of 0.10, net benefit was +0.028 pre-pandemic and +0.058 post-pandemic, the external values computed after intercept/slope updating, against +0.010 internally, in each case above screen-all (-0.017, +0.024 and -0.072) and above screen-none (zero by definition). The operating threshold was fixed once in the development data at the point giving 85% sensitivity and then applied unchanged to the hold-out and to both external cohorts; hence the external operating characteristics are transported values rather than values guaranteed by construction. At that single threshold, sensitivity/specificity were 0.833/0.665 internally, 0.810/0.566 in NHANES pre and 0.791/0.596 in NHANES post, with positive predictive value 0.147 and 0.214 externally (number needed to screen 6.8 and 4.7) and negative predictive value 0.970 and 0.953; the model flagged 47% of the pre-pandemic and 45% of the post-pandemic population. The flagged share is a function of where the threshold is set rather than a fixed property of the model: at the decision-curve thresholds of 0.10 and 0.15 it falls to 27% and 16% pre-pandemic and to 40% and 26% post-pandemic (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S3). Full operating characteristics and net benefit are in Multimedia Appendix 1, Tables S1, S2 and S3. Clinical utility remains provisional pending prospective evaluation.

## Discussion

### Principal Findings

A depression-screening ML model developed in Korea and externally validated in the US met its internal target (AUROC 0.836) and retained clinically useful, though lower, discrimination (~0.77) on genuinely external US data and across the COVID-19 shift, with calibration recoverable by lightweight recalibration. Further modeling did not raise external discrimination, and the plateau is not attributable to a deficiency of the algorithm.

First, external discrimination was 0.765 (95% CI 0.724-0.804) before the pandemic and 0.771 (0.739-0.801) after it; the difference is +0.006 (95% CI -0.045 to +0.056; bootstrap P=.86); the two cycles are therefore not distinguishable at this sample size. That interval was computed after the fact, no between-cycle test having been pre-specified. Second, calibration behaved as transportability theory predicts: the slope fell to 0.65 and 0.68 and calibration-in-the-large rose to +0.51 and +1.13, meaning the Korea-trained model systematically under-predicted absolute risk in a higher-prevalence population, and intercept-plus-slope updating restored both without re-estimating any predictor effect, to slope 1.00 (0.95-1.00) and 1.00 (0.97-1.00), with the update refitted inside every replicate so that its own uncertainty is carried. Third, a six-item form selected entirely inside the development data lost only 0.015 internally and 0.018 and 0.012 externally, which places a low-burden self-report instrument within reach for digital triage. Whether triage delivered this way improves depression outcomes was not evaluated here; the mobile-delivery evidence base concerns intervention delivery to defined patient groups [40] rather than the delivery of screening, and no equivalent trial evidence exists for a transported screening algorithm. With the threshold fixed once in the development data at 85% sensitivity and then applied unchanged, external sensitivity was 0.810 and 0.791 with a number needed to screen of 6.8 and 4.7, and decision-curve net benefit exceeded both screen-all and screen-none defaults in every dataset, with the external cohorts taken after local recalibration.

### Comparison With Prior Work

Prior ML depression models built on Korean national surveys report strong internal performance but no external validation. Using KNHANES 2014-2018 restricted to non-smokers at risk of secondhand smoke exposure (n=11,463), Kim et al [6] classified PHQ-9 ≥10 with internal AUROC up to 0.90; using the 2023 KCHS restricted to culturally diverse families (n=2,568), Kim et al [5] predicted the continuous PHQ-9 score with a test R² of 33%. Two deliberate design choices plausibly account for the lower internal discrimination (0.836), although no ablation was run to attribute the difference quantitatively across studies that differ in population and outcome scale: outcome-proximal predictors such as perceived stress and single-item depressed-mood questions (which inflate apparent performance but are near-tautological with the outcome) were excluded; and the predictor set was restricted to variables transportable to a second country. The wider field supplies the same contrast in aggregate: a systematic review of machine-learning depression models built on electronic health records found reported discrimination clustered near 0.80, almost always internal [7], and among hundreds of prediction models published during the pandemic almost none were validated outside the development source [8]. An internal 0.836 is therefore unremarkable against that literature; an externally validated 0.77 in a second country has few published counterparts.

In exchange, this study provides cross-national and cross-pandemic external validation, an explicit transportability ceiling, and a recalibration analysis. That the same predictors carry substantially more signal within Korea (0.836 internally, 0.81-0.82 at matched sample size) than in the US (~0.77) is a concrete instance of the dataset-shift problem described for deployed clinical artificial intelligence [11,41], and underscores that high internally reported AUROCs do not guarantee cross-population usefulness.

### Plausible Mechanisms for the External Ceiling

Several lines of evidence converge on concept shift as the leading explanation for the ~0.77 external plateau. The first rules out modeling effort. Across 2,199 catalogued runs spanning feature engineering, hyperparameter search, ensembling, domain adaptation, alternative losses and deep tabular learners, no configuration selected on development-internal criteria exceeded that level, and apparent gains from externally tuned variants did not survive leakage control, leakage being the failure mode that has driven reproducibility problems across machine-learning-based science [42]; runs screened on external labels are identified as such in Multimedia Appendix 1. The second rules out estimation error: matching sample size did not close the gap, and increasing the training set from 0.5 to 2 million records slightly lowered external discrimination, a pattern consistent with source-specific overfitting rather than an estimation-limited ceiling.

The most direct test was an in-domain oracle, made for diagnosis only. Fitting NHANES on its own labels under cluster-separated cross-validation reached just 0.764-0.776, whereas the same predictors reached 0.81-0.82 in equally sized Korean samples. The transported model therefore already performs at the level reached by the best model that could be fitted on those predictors inside the external population, among the learners evaluated.

At a PHQ-9 ≥5 threshold the Korea-US discrimination gap narrows from about 0.07 to about 0.02 (0.761 internally against 0.735 and 0.740 externally), and restricting training and evaluation to adults aged ≥30 years narrows it from 0.071 and 0.066 to 0.058 and 0.051. Both patterns place the shortfall at the moderate-or-worse end of the symptom distribution and among the youngest adults, and are suggestive rather than confirmatory.

Interpretability points the same way. Predictor-importance rank order was almost unchanged between surveys (Spearman ρ=0.97 pre-pandemic and 0.99 post-pandemic), indicating that the transported model draws on the predictors in a similar order in both populations. Near-identical ordering alongside external discrimination about 0.07 lower is more consistent with predictors that are the right ones but weaker externally than with a model attending to the wrong ones. That is a statement about the fixed model's attribution and not about the external predictor-outcome associations, which the attribution never observes; it is a pattern that is at least consistent with the shift-stability framing of deployment failure in health artificial intelligence [43]. Measurement differences and complete-case selection cannot be separated from concept shift with these data.

### Clinical and Policy Implications

The intended use is triage, not diagnosis; a positive score prompts confirmatory assessment rather than an action in itself. At the single development-fixed operating point, positive predictive value was 0.147 in the pre-pandemic cohort and 0.214 in the post-pandemic cohort, ie, 6.8 and 4.7 respondents screened per case identified, and the model flagged 47% and 45% of the population for confirmatory assessment; net benefit exceeded both default strategies across the clinically relevant threshold range. The flag rate, not the positive predictive value, is what a screening program must size its confirmatory capacity against.

All six items (self-rated health, age, sex, sleep duration, self-rated oral health, and partnership) are answerable on a single screen and require no examination, no laboratory value and no clinician; none is a symptom item, which keeps the triage step distinct from the full PHQ-9 it is meant to prioritize. The 17-predictor version additionally needs body mass index and two physician-diagnosed conditions, which a self-completed form can only take on trust.

Any implementation outside the development population must apply local recalibration, since discrimination transported but absolute risk did not; intercept-plus-slope updating restored calibration in both external cohorts, each using that cohort's full sample, and is the recommended update; the local sample size needed for a stable update was not examined here; published minimum-sample-size derivations for external validation are driven by the calibration slope rather than the AUROC [44], and that constraint binds first at a small deployment site.

Subgroup discrimination was lowest in Mexican American respondents in both NHANES cycles and, within the development population, in the highest income quintile, which is the one development subgroup that trips the analysis plan's 0.05 flag. The NHANES race and ethnicity intervals are wide and mutually overlapping, which leaves those differences unestablished. Either way, a deployed screener should report subgroup performance continuously rather than assume the development-time estimate holds, as recommended for machine learning intended to advance rather than erode health equity [45]. Three quantities warrant tracking: the subgroup AUROC, the subgroup calibration slope, and the false-negative rate at the operating threshold, which spans 0.03 to 0.31 across the subgroups reported here (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S7).

### Strengths and Limitations

The principal strength is that the outcome is identical in both surveys. Depression is defined as a nine-item PHQ-9 sum of at least 10 in KCHS and in NHANES alike, which removes the outcome-harmonization error that usually confounds cross-national validation and leaves country, health system, racial and ethnic composition, and survey mode as the differences under test. Evaluation was complex-survey weighted throughout, and a data-integrity audit and subsequent peer review corrected seven data-processing and harmonization defects before the primary analysis. The optimization search was leakage-controlled, which removes external-label selection as a source of optimism in the ceiling claim.

Depression-module non-response within the mobile examination center estimation frame cost 7.7% of the pre-pandemic and 14.1% of the post-pandemic sample, counting any unanswered item (14.6% and 33.3% on the wider interview frame; Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S8), and was handled as complete-case; given that non-response was differential by education and by race and ethnicity, the external estimates, and particularly the post-pandemic subgroup results, may be biased in an unknown direction. Alcohol, physical activity, income, body mass index and food insecurity are measured differently in the two surveys and were harmonized to conservatively comparable definitions. Every predictor value used here was collected by interviewer administration or in an examination center; a web or mobile form changes the mode of administration for all 17 items, and the effect of that change on the reported performance is untested. The design is cross-sectional; hence the model screens current symptoms rather than predicting future onset. Living alone is unavailable in the pre-pandemic NHANES cycle, and food insecurity is a single item in KCHS and unmeasured there in 2020 and 2021. Prior depression diagnosis is not recorded in KCHS, which precluded a diagnosis-exclusion sensitivity analysis.

Development used a 500,000-record training set and a 100,001-record hold-out drawn from the 2,048,510 pooled records rather than the full pooled sample; a training-set size experiment showed this did not cost external discrimination, but internal estimates are hold-out rather than fully cross-validated. The external cohorts carry 728 and 661 events, modest for a validation study and most limiting for calibration; the calibration slope is typically the binding precision constraint when the minimum sample size for external validation of a binary-outcome model is derived [44]; conditioning on a single fitted update rather than refitting it gives visibly wider slope intervals (0.82-1.19 pre and 0.88-1.13 post, Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S16); a deployment that fits its update once on a modest local sample is in that wider regime. The amendment that added three of the seventeen predictors was written while the study was under way, after an earlier external result was known but before any model using the amended set was fitted; it is dated, summarized in English in Multimedia Appendix 1 with the unmodified Korean original released alongside the code, and its contribution is reported as a robustness analysis rather than a primary result. Finally, two of the four hypotheses, H2 and H3, were stated qualitatively in the protocol, without numeric decision margins; thus judging those two "supported" involves interpretation.

A competing contributor is partly quantified, and it is not concept shift: the transportable predictor set is bounded by the development survey's content. In a coverage analysis, candidate domains available in NHANES but not harmonizable to KCHS were extracted: physician-diagnosed multimorbidity (asthma, arthritis, thyroid, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, liver disease, and cancer, excluding circulatory conditions) and healthcare utilization; adding those domains raised the NHANES in-domain oracle from 0.765 to 0.778 (Multimedia Appendix 1, Table S14). KCHS, however, records only hypertension and diabetes among physician-diagnosed conditions across more than one survey year, both already included (dyslipidemia, osteoporosis, cataract and arthritis are fielded in a single year and therefore cannot enter a model pooled over 2017-2025), and does not collect a broader chronic-disease inventory, hospitalization, or visit counts; these domains therefore cannot enter a KCHS-developed transportable model. Part of the residual external gap is thus attributable to development-survey coverage rather than to concept shift alone, and a development survey that captured multimorbidity and utilization might raise the attainable ceiling, though the 0.013 increment observed here is an in-domain NHANES gain and is not itself evidence that the added signal would transport from Korea.

### Conclusions

A survey-derived depression screener transports across country and pandemic with clinically useful discrimination and recoverable calibration. The cross-national ceiling near 0.77 held against every leakage-controlled attempt to exceed it and against an in-domain oracle fitted on the external labels. It is therefore best read as a property of the transportable predictor set rather than as a proven maximum, most consistent with shift in the predictor-outcome relationship and with residual contributions from differing measurement, development-survey coverage and complete-case selection. Remaining gains for a screener of this kind are more likely to come from predictor domains a development survey does not yet collect than from further algorithm search. A low-burden six-item version, paired with local recalibration and continuing subgroup monitoring, is a plausible scalable digital triage aid.

KCHS microdata are access-controlled and were obtained through the KDCA raw-data request-and-review process (https://chs.kdca.go.kr); under the data-use agreement the microdata cannot be redistributed. Analysis code sufficient to refit the reported model exactly from the source data (fixed seed, fixed hyperparameters), the full preprocessing specification (including the household income quintile, which is derived within the target cohort before the fitted pipeline is applied and is therefore not reproducible from a single individual's record alone), the variable-harmonization dictionary, the model card, and the protocol, statistical analysis plan and amendment documents are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request, subject to applicable survey data-use restrictions; derived result tables are provided as Multimedia Appendices.
